# LinkedIn Series 2/5: Downs Positional Value

Status: draft, ready to paste
Source: published/nfl-draft/2026-04-05 downs_positional_value.md ("What makes a consensus, elite player slide? Caleb Downs is about to find out.")

---

In April, I published a model finding that the NFL was about to discount one of the best players in the draft over his position label. On draft night, it played out almost exactly as written.

The player was Caleb Downs, the consensus elite safety with a Hall of Fame coach on record saying he'd take him "in a second." The market still projected a slide to picks 10 through 12, because safety isn't a premium position. Our model's read of 15 years of first-round history said the discount is a tax on the position name, not a risk premium. First-round safeties bust at 17.2%. The positions teams draft in the top 10 without blinking run worse: 25.0% for quarterbacks, 23.9% for edge rushers, 27.1% for cornerbacks. The league absorbs a one-in-four failure rate at "premium" spots, then calls one-in-six too risky at safety.

The method: draft-slot-adjusted four-year Approximate Value for every first-round pick from 2006 through 2020, with boom and bust defined as significant over- or underperformance against what each position historically produces at that slot. One modeling decision did the most work. The original design pooled corners and safeties as "defensive backs," and their outcomes canceled out: corners boom at 4%, safeties at 12%. Splitting them is what surfaced the argument.

The receipt: Downs went 11th, and Dallas traded up to get him. A top-five talent at a mid-first price created entirely by positional prejudice, four years after Kyle Hamilton slid to 14th and became the highest-paid safety in history.

The takeaway travels beyond football: aggregation choices are arguments. Pool the wrong categories and your model inherits the market's blind spot instead of exposing it.
