# Twelve Points. Fifty-Three Years.

---

San Antonio led by double digits in every game of this series. They outscored the Knicks in the first quarter by 57 points across five games. On a pure first-quarter record, they were the better basketball team.

They still lost 4-1.

Put the Wembanyama ceiling takes down. The 2026 NBA Finals were decided by fourth-quarter personnel math, and San Antonio ran out of it every time it mattered. Understanding how a team leads Q1 of five consecutive Finals games and still loses the series is the only honest accounting of what just happened.

## The Number Nobody Will Talk About

The New York Knicks won the NBA championship by a combined 12 points across five games — 10, 1, minus-4, 1, and 4. Average winning margin: 2.4 points per game.

Not all 4-1 series wins are the same.

[TABLE IMAGE: finals_historical_margins.png]

Every other 3-1 lead in a Finals series in the last twelve years was held by a team winning games by at least six points on average. The only team to blow a 3-1 Finals lead — the 2016 Warriors — was winning by 7.3 points per game through four games. The Knicks were winning by 2.0. Two of their three wins were decided by a single basket. One of those required the largest single-game comeback in NBA Finals history — down 29 in the first half, won by one on an Anunoby tip at 1.2 seconds.

The 95 percent close-out rate on 3-1 leads is real. The certainty that gets attached to it isn't.

## Why the Spurs Couldn't Close It

Here is the structure of this series, stripped to quarters.

First quarter: San Antonio. Every game. They led by 8 at the end of Q1, then 9, then 11, then 19, then 10. Double-digit peak lead in all five games. They were the team that came out ready every single night.

Fourth quarter: New York. The Knicks outscored the Spurs by 26 points in Q4 across the series. They won the fourth quarter in four of five games, and in the one game they lost it — Game 2 — they'd already built enough of a lead that an eight-point Spurs comeback wasn't enough.

The formula we outlined before the series was right, just not in the direction the Spurs needed. When Castle scores 17 or fewer, he has defensive reserves. When Castle tries to score and stop Brunson in the same fourth quarter, something gives. In Game 5 he went 1-for-10 from the field — six points — while Brunson scored 45. KAT fouled out with two points and five turnovers. The Spurs led by 7 entering the fourth and got outscored 29-18.

That's not a Wembanyama problem. That's a personnel structure problem. And it has a clear solution.

## What the Losing Team Showed Us

A pre-draft scouting report on Dylan Harper stated plainly that he "didn't show much resistance at the point of attack and will need to improve his effort and awareness."

In Game 5 of the NBA Finals, Harper was the best point defender the Knicks faced on Brunson all series. He tied Brunson up on drives. He stayed in front. Castle, who couldn't find his shot all night, was eventually pulled from the game while Harper was still producing at both ends. The scout was watching a 19-year-old on a 15-17 Rutgers team in February. Projecting point-of-attack defense from that sample to guarding a Finals MVP in June is one of the hardest calls in draft evaluation. That particular one aged about as well as calling him a non-shooter — he hit shots all series long.

Harper is 20 years old. He became the fourth rookie in NBA history to score 20 or more points in consecutive Finals games, joining Alvan Adams (1976), Tom Heinsohn (1957), and Joe Fulks (1947). The youngest player ever to score 20-plus in a Finals game. He set the Spurs franchise record for rookie points in a single postseason, passing David Robinson's 243 from 1990.

His Finals line: 16, 15, 13, 21, 25. The trajectory matters as much as the average.

The counterintuitive wrinkle: his worst shooting game was Game 3 — the only Spurs win. His two best games were losses. When Harper was excellent, Castle had defensive reserves. When Harper went cold, Castle tried to compensate, spent himself doing it, and handed Brunson the space he needed. The co-star dependency ran the other direction from what the series box score implies.

His father Ron Harper won five championships as a role player for Jordan's Bulls and Shaq's early Lakers. Dylan grew up understanding what June basketball requires. They drafted a kid out of a 15-17 Rutgers team with the second pick. They may have found the piece that wins them titles more than the one they already have.

## How You Actually Build One of These

Fifty-three years. Zero lottery cornerstones.

Jalen Brunson came from Dallas in free agency. OG Anunoby came from Toronto in a trade. Josh Hart came from Portland in a trade. Mikal Bridges came from Brooklyn in a trade. Karl-Anthony Towns came from Minnesota in a trade. The team that just ended the longest drought in major North American professional sports didn't bottom out for a supermax pick. They were better at acquiring good players than everyone else in the room, then built a culture that could erase a 29-point deficit in a road Finals game and find a way.

There is no glamorous thesis here. No draft lottery moment, no singular night when the franchise turned. Just sustained front-office execution across years — reading the market, moving assets at the right time, assembling a roster greater than the sum of its parts. Every front office that bottomed out chasing a generational pick can now point to this roster and ask whether the plan was actually optimal. Some of them took the right pick and still waited a decade.

Wembanyama is 22. When Harper is ready to be the co-star every night — and the trajectory in this series says he's close — this team becomes a different problem for whoever they play in June. The Spurs will be back. Not because of Wemby's ceiling, but because of who's standing next to him.

The Knicks earned this. Every close win. Every tipped ball. Fifty-three years ended 12 points at a time, and that's the most New York way it could have gone.
