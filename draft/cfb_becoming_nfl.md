# Toto, I've A Feeling We're Not In Kansas Anymore

Every week I flip a switch in my content folder from college football to the NFL and back again. It's literally one line in one file, and lately I'm not sure it matters. College players change teams every offseason like free agents. The 12-team playoff hands automatic bids to conference champions, the same way the NFL is about to hand one to whoever survives the NFC South. And the AP poll keeps ranking records that the numbers say are lying, which is exactly what we spent last week doing to the Vikings. So I went looking for whether college football is actually turning into the NFL, or just wearing its jersey.

Short answer: college has built the NFL's house. It hasn't moved the NFL's parity in yet.

## You Get A Car! You Get A Car!

The NFL's parity comes from the draft and the salary cap spreading good players around. College has neither, but it's starting to get a similar result anyway.

247Sports' talent composite scores every roster by the recruiting ratings of its players, transfers included. In 2022, the five most talented rosters averaged 161 points more than the teams ranked 6th through 25th in the composite. By 2025 that gap was down to 119, the smallest it's been, and it's 127 this year. The top didn't get worse. The middle class got better: the top five used to rate 1.45 times the teams ranked 26th to 50th in talent, and now it's 1.33 times. You can see it at the top of the poll, where three of the AP top 7 don't even have a top-35 roster by talent: Indiana is 38th in the composite, Utah 58th and BYU 59th.

The betting market sees it too. The point spread is how many points the market expects the favorite to win by, and in games between two FBS teams the typical spread was about 10 points from 2014 to 2019. Since 2023, it's been 7.5 to 8.5, and it's 8.5 this year. The NFL's typical spread is 3.5, so college isn't there yet, but it's heading that way. (One caveat: the 2024 conference realignment put more power-conference teams on each other's schedules, which shrinks spreads by itself, and nobody has pulled the two effects apart.) Want to see it? This Saturday, #1 Miami is a 4.5-point underdog at #4 Notre Dame, and #2 Georgia is laying just 3 at #12 Ole Miss.

So if the matchups are getting closer, does that mean more upsets? Not the way you'd think. At the same spread, college underdogs already win more often than NFL underdogs. From 2014 to 2025, teams getting 7 to 13.5 points won 25.8% of the time in college and 21.8% in the NFL, and college is right on its average at 25.5% this year. "Any given Sunday" doesn't mean bad NFL teams beat good ones more often. It means NFL games start close. NFL favorites win about 64% of the time, college favorites about 76%. College will get more chaotic as the spreads keep shrinking, not because a 10-point underdog suddenly found its courage.

## Show Me The Money!

If talent is spreading out, the next question is how it's moving. The NFL has free agency. College has the transfer portal, and they're starting to look like the same thing. Portal entries went from 1,770 in 2021 to 4,499 in 2025, then flattened at 4,484 this year, the first year without growth. That's also the first year of the new 105-man roster limits, which is a good guess at the reason, but only a guess.

What makes it a market and not a fire sale is that players find buyers. About 4 in 5 entrants now land at a new school, up from 3 in 5 in 2021, and the median school taking transfers went from 3 to 11. Oklahoma State took 55. Fifty-five! That's not a recruiting class, that's an expansion draft.

Like the NFL, there's more than one way to build a contender with it. UCLA is 8-0 and #5 in the AP poll with 43 incoming transfers this cycle, the most of anyone in the AP top 7. The blue bloods shop differently. Georgia took 9 transfers and Notre Dame took 7, both in the bottom 6% of the 69 power-conference teams (Notre Dame included) by count. But the Bulldogs, the Irish and Ohio State all rank in the top 20% for four- and five-star transfers. The Bruins are filling out the roster in bulk. Georgia only shops on the top shelf.

What the portal isn't is the reason the top teams keep losing. The six teams that have beaten a #1 or #2 this year took a median of 20 transfers, right next to the 21.5 for power-conference teams overall. It changed how teams get built. It didn't make the top teams beatable.

## If He's A Good Hitter, Why Doesn't He Hit Good?

Last week I said if the Vikings couldn't score on Detroit's defense, their 6-0 record had a ceiling. Well, we found it. Minnesota lost 19-15 in Detroit on Sunday, the first time all year it faced a team the market rated as roughly its equal (the Vikings were 3-point underdogs). The defense is still 1st in EPA per play allowed, the offense is still 29th in EPA per play, and five of the six wins came by 10 points or fewer against opponents with a combined 17-25 record. That doesn't make the Vikings a fraud. They're still about 6th in net EPA per play, which is a good team. Just not the team 6-0 said they were.

College football has that problem every single week, because the AP poll mostly ranks records the way NFL standings do. The computers rank teams. To compare the two, I lined the poll up against strength of record, which grades how impressive a record is given who a team played, and a computer consensus: each team's median rank across six measures (point differential, SRS, Elo, FPI, SP+ and EPA per play) out of 138 FBS teams.

[TABLE IMAGE: AP rank vs. computer consensus and strength of record -- Kentucky, New Mexico, BYU, Boise State, Florida, UCLA (poll higher); LSU, Ohio State, Arizona (poll lower)]

Kentucky is college's Vikings. The Wildcats are 7-1 and 8th in the AP poll, and their record is the 2nd most impressive in the country by strength of record. Every efficiency measure ranks them somewhere between 14th and 49th, and their median computer rank is 36.5. Even unbeaten UCLA, 5th in the AP poll, only has a median computer rank of 15. New Mexico shows why the schedule matters: the Lobos' point differential is 5th in the country, but adjusted for who they played they're 45th in SRS (margin of victory adjusted for opponents).

Then there's the mirror image. LSU is 6-2 and 15th in the AP poll, but 1st in SRS and 1st in EPA per play, with a median computer rank of 3.5. Ohio State is 6-2 and 11th in the AP poll, but 1st in SP+ and 2nd in FPI, with a median computer rank of 6. SP+ and FPI still carry some preseason weighting this time of year, so you could argue the Buckeyes are getting a little credit for being Ohio State. LSU doesn't have that excuse. SRS and EPA per play are built entirely from this season's games, and the Tigers lead both. If I'm picking an opponent in December, I'm taking 7-1 Kentucky over 6-2 LSU or Ohio State, and it isn't close.

## If You Ain't First, You're Last

Ricky Bobby never played in the NFC South. The whole division has a losing record: Carolina leads at 3-4, Tampa Bay and Atlanta are 3-5 and New Orleans is 2-5. It's the only division in the league without a team over .500, and one of them is getting a home playoff game.

If you're picking one, pick a team that's currently tied for second. Tampa Bay is 3-5 with a +27 point differential and ranks 10th in net EPA per play, behind the league's 2nd-best defense by EPA per play allowed. The other three range from 17th (Carolina) to 27th (New Orleans) in net EPA. The Bucs are the only team in the division playing like a playoff team.

The NFC South has been here before. Since the 2002 realignment, seven division winners have finished .500 or worse, and three came from the NFC South, including last year's 8-9 Panthers. Those teams didn't just show up and lose, though. Four of the seven won their home wild-card game, including 7-9 Seattle over the defending champion Saints in 2010 and Tim Tebow's 8-8 Broncos over Pittsburgh in 2011. All four lost the next week, but a bad NFL division winner is still an NFL team hosting another NFL team.

College football copied the structure. The 12-team playoff guarantees spots to the highest-ranked conference champions, and in both 12-team fields so far, the lowest-rated teams by season's end were champions with automatic bids:

- **2024:** Boise State (12-1, 30th in SRS, 29th in SP+), Arizona State (11-2, 16th in SRS, 35th in SP+) and Clemson (10-3, 23rd in SRS, 22nd in SP+).
- **2025:** Tulane (11-2, 56th in SRS, 45th in SP+) and James Madison (12-1, 29th in SRS, 27th in SP+).

Those five went 0-5 and lost by about 17 points a game, from Texas' 39-31 win over Arizona State to Ole Miss' 41-10 win over Tulane. Weak NFL division winners won four of their seven home playoff games. Weak college champions haven't won one. College took the NFL's automatic bids without the NFL's parity at the top.

This year's leading candidates live in the Mountain West, where New Mexico (7-1, 22nd in the AP poll, median computer rank of 36.5) and Boise State (7-1, 20th in the AP poll, median computer rank of 32) can't both win the title, and in the Sun Belt, where James Madison is unranked but has a median computer rank of 25. Whoever comes out of the Mountain West is my pick to be this year's one-and-done. Whoever comes out of the NFC South, especially if it's Tampa Bay, has a better shot at a playoff win than any of them.

---

College football has the NFL's skeleton now: a free-agent market, automatic bids for weak champions and a poll that grades the standings. What it doesn't have yet is the NFL's parity. The middle class is closing the gap and the spreads are shrinking with it, but the weak champions are still getting run off the field in December. We're not in Kansas anymore. We're just not in the NFL yet either.
