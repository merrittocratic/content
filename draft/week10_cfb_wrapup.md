# Life Is A Game Of Inches

I've been saying it all week to anyone who will listen: two or three plays decide most big games. Saturday in Columbus, I got to watch it happen to my own team. Ohio State led Oregon 14-3 late in the first half, and then the Ducks scored 35 straight points. It took about 13 minutes of game clock for the game to flip. In Lexington, a 13-6 game turned into a 34-6 blowout one turnover at a time. In Baton Rouge, LSU got inside Alabama's 40 eight times and scored 13 points. And in South Bend, Miami came within 18 yards of tying a game that flipped the playoff conversation on its head.

## Life Moves Pretty Fast

Oregon 41, Ohio State 28. After the field goal that made it 14-3, Ohio State's win probability peaked at 88%. It was 35% at halftime and 15% after Oregon's first drive of the third quarter. No single play did that. The biggest swing on the day was 15 points of win probability. It took six or seven plays, each worth 5 to 15 points, all landing on Oregon's side in one stretch. Three of them could easily have gone the other way.

Take them one at a time, because each one changes everything that comes after it:

- **Jay Timmons, first quarter.** Ohio State was up 7-0 with about 3:40 left, and Oregon faced 3rd-and-9 from its own 40. Timmons broke on the throw with nothing but green grass in front of him, and couldn't hang on. A pick-six makes it 14-0, and Ohio State scored a touchdown on its very next drive anyway. At 21-0 after one quarter, Ohio State's win probability is about 98%. Every FBS team that has led by 21 or more after a quarter this season has won. Instead, it was an incompletion and a punt.
- **Jaylen McClain, late in the second quarter.** Ohio State was up 14-3 with about 2:30 left in the half, and Oregon faced another 3rd-and-9, this time from its own 31. McClain dropped it. On the very next snap, linebacker Teitum Tuioti threw a fake punt 30 yards to Jalen Lott, and seven plays later Dierre Hill Jr. scored to make it 14-10. A pick there gives Ohio State the ball near midfield, up 11, with its win probability around 90%.
- **Luke Fahey, 1:06 left in the half.** This is the one that should sting the most. Before Saturday, Ohio State had scored a touchdown on 6 of its 7 drives that started in the last four minutes of a half, the best rate in FBS. The Power 4 average is 19%. This time it was three plays, 14 yards and an interception by Oregon's Devin Jackson at the Oregon 41. Then the Buckeye defense, which hadn't allowed a touchdown on a late first-half drive all year, was flagged for holding twice on the same drive: Earl Little Jr. on 3rd-and-7, and Dominick Kelly at the 3 with seven seconds left. The second hold gave Oregon an untimed down, and Jamari Johnson scored at 0:00 to make it 17-14 Oregon. Score the usual touchdown instead and Ohio State goes to the locker room up 21-10, with about an 87% chance to win. Teams up 8 to 14 at halftime this season won 83% of the time. Ohio State's actual halftime spot, a big pregame favorite trailing by 1 to 7, has ended in a win only 57% of the time.

Any one of those three plays, and Ohio State is somewhere between 87% and 98% to win this game. That's what I mean by two or three plays. Hill then opened the third quarter with a 48-yard touchdown run on 2nd-and-13 to make it 24-14, and the rest of the game was the Buckeyes chasing.

Which brings us to the third quarter, and for Ohio State that isn't news. On Saturday, the offense managed -0.76 EPA per play and a 20% success rate in the third, and scored 0 points while Oregon scored 14. Over the season, the Buckeyes score 4.6 points per game in the third quarter, 104th in FBS in third-quarter scoring, against 11.4 in the first quarter, 3rd in FBS. Per play, the third-quarter offense is at -0.25 EPA, 132nd of 138 in FBS. They've been shut out in the third in 3 of 9 games. It's the same shape as the Week 2 collapse at Texas: 3 points in the third, none in the fourth, and 21 allowed.

The box score will tell you Fahey had a big day, 36 of 50 for 360 yards and two touchdowns. It's lying. While the game was within 13 points, he was 14 of 23 for 138 yards, no touchdowns and the interception, at -0.34 EPA per dropback. Both touchdowns came with Ohio State down 24 and 19. Jeremiah Smith's 7 catches for 133 yards tell the same story: before Ohio State fell behind 31-14, he'd been targeted 4 times for 46 yards. And for the first time all season, someone out-targeted him. Devin McCuin drew 14 targets to Smith's 10, and turned them into 84 yards and -7.7 EPA, the worst of any Ohio State receiver. In Julian Sayin's starts, Smith got 33% of the targets and McCuin 12%. In Fahey's two starts, it's 26% and 23%. Whoever is playing quarterback, the ball needs to find the best player on the field.

## You Can't Handle The Truth!

In my piece on college football turning into the NFL, I used Georgia at Ole Miss as an example of shrinking point spreads: #2 Georgia was laying just 3 points at #10 Ole Miss in the CFP rankings. Georgia won 42-7. A close spread doesn't promise a close game, and this one was 28-0 at halftime. The Bulldogs ran for 294 yards at 6.1 per carry, Ole Miss ran for 66 at 2.1 and gave up 5 sacks, and Trinidad Chambliss finished with -12 rushing yards. Gunner Stockton only needed 9 completions, for 186 yards and 4 touchdowns, and Talyn Taylor caught 3 of them, including scores of 58 and 65 yards. Before the game got out of hand, Georgia was at +0.48 EPA per play and Ole Miss at -0.29. Georgia is now 1st in net EPA (offensive efficiency minus defensive efficiency allowed), 3rd in defensive EPA and 2nd in SP+. The only asterisk is that Ole Miss's defense was already 118th in EPA against the run, so 294 rushing yards was the matchup doing what you'd expect.

The more interesting SEC result was in Lexington. In that same piece, I called Kentucky college's version of the Minnesota Vikings: 7-1 and 6th in the CFP rankings, with a record that's 2nd in the country by strength of record and a median computer rank of 36.5. I said I'd rather face 7-1 Kentucky in December than 6-2 LSU or Ohio State. Tennessee won 34-6.

The final score oversells it, though. It was 13-6 after three quarters, and the game swung on Kentucky turnovers: an interception in the Tennessee end zone in the first quarter, a Jovantae Barnes fumble at midfield in the second, and a Kenny Minchey strip-sack at the Kentucky 47 with 30 seconds left in the third. Tennessee scored on the next drive and finished with 21 points in the fourth, while Kentucky's last real chances ended in fourth-down sacks. Kentucky ran more plays, 69 to 52, and Minchey completed 26 of 34 passes. It was empty: 224 yards on an average throw of 6.7 yards downfield, and -0.38 EPA per dropback, 97th of 108 quarterbacks this week. Tennessee only needed 10 completions because it ran for 240 yards at 6.5 per carry.

That's what the computers were saying all along. At 7-2, the Wildcats are 58th in net EPA, 60th in offensive EPA and 69th in defensive EPA. Tennessee's defense, 11th in defensive EPA and 6th in EPA against the pass, is exactly the kind of unit that exposes a dink-and-dunk passing game. Like the Vikings in Detroit, Kentucky finally ran into a team that could do the one thing it couldn't, and the record caught up with the numbers.

The other two teams in that sentence lost on Saturday too, and LSU's loss is the one the computers have to answer for. Alabama, 5th in the CFP rankings, won 31-13 in Baton Rouge, which sounds like a beating. It wasn't. LSU ran 81 plays to Alabama's 52 and had the better success rate, 47% to 40%, and the per-play numbers were close: Alabama at +0.03 EPA per play, LSU at -0.08. It was 17-13 entering the fourth quarter.

LSU just couldn't finish. Between its own 20 and Alabama's 40, it was excellent: +0.29 EPA per play and a 61% success rate. Inside Alabama's 40, it fell apart: -0.51 EPA per play and a 30% success rate, its worst game of the season inside the 40. The Tigers got there eight times and came away with 13 points: two field goals, one touchdown, a missed 56-yarder, an interception and three turnovers on downs. Alabama got inside LSU's 40 six times and scored 31. LSU had gone 10 of 11 on fourth down before Saturday. It went 0 for 4, including at the Alabama 9 down 10-3 and at the Alabama 7.

Alabama got the rest on short fields. Ryan Coleman-Williams' 32-yard punt return set up a 25-yard touchdown drive, and LSU's failed fourth down at its own 25 set up an 18-yard one. That's 14 of Alabama's 31 points, and a 17-13 game became 31-13 in about six minutes of game clock. Keelon Russell only needed 17 completions for 206 yards, but threw 3 touchdowns at +0.16 EPA per dropback, 34th of 108 quarterbacks this week. Like Fahey's, Sam Leavitt's box score flatters him, for a different reason. He completed 35 passes for 380 yards, 11 of them to Jackson Harris for 166, but the yards came between the 40s. With no touchdowns, an interception and 4 sacks, he finished at -0.04 EPA per dropback, 63rd of 108.

So were the computers wrong about LSU? No. Even after the loss, the Tigers are 3rd in SP+, 3rd in SRS and 3rd in net EPA. Alabama moved up to 1st in SRS, but sits 7th in SP+ and 12th in net EPA. LSU's finishing problem isn't new, just usually smaller: over the season, it's 19th in EPA per play between the 40s and 53rd inside the 40. Saturday was the extreme version. Kentucky was a record lying about the team. LSU is a team whose efficiency is real, but the computers grade all 100 yards of the field, and the scoreboard only pays at the end of a drive.

## Revenge Is A Dish Best Served Cold

Notre Dame 31, Miami 24. The Irish led 24-3 early in the third quarter. Miami came back to 24-17 and then 31-24, and turned it over on downs at the Notre Dame 18 with 3:36 left.

The pregame storyline was Malachi Toney. He'd drawn 80 of Miami's 267 targets in eight games, 30% and more than twice anyone else, and he wasn't targeted once on Saturday. The raw numbers say Miami missed him: its EPA per dropback fell from 0.45 in games with Toney to 0.18. But Cooper Barkate (10 catches, 118 yards, 2 touchdowns) and Elija Lofton (7 for 113 and a touchdown) were productive without him, and once you account for a Notre Dame pass defense that ranks 35th in pass-defense EPA, Saturday was only Miami's 3rd-worst passing day of the season relative to its opponent. Two games with Toney, Florida State and Wake Forest, were worse.

The bigger story is on the other side of the ball. Back in Week 6, I said Miami's pass defense was the weak spot to watch when a real opponent that can throw the ball finally shows up. It showed up. C.J. Carr completed 29 passes for 319 yards, 3 touchdowns and no interceptions, at +0.26 EPA per dropback and a completion percentage 10.8 points above expected. Miami's pass defense is now 126th of 138 in EPA allowed, even though it's 3rd in EPA against the run. The Hurricanes' offense is 2nd in offensive EPA, and their defense is 78th in defensive EPA. That's a team that has to outscore people.

All week, the conversation was whether a loss would knock Notre Dame out of the playoff. Notre Dame finally has its signature win, over the committee's #1 team, and at 11th in the CFP rankings it gets an automatic bid if it finishes in the top 12. So flip the question: what if Miami loses again?

- **Lose once more and still win the ACC:** Miami is in. The Hurricanes are 6-0 in the ACC with home games left against Duke, Virginia Tech and Boston College, and they already own the tiebreaker over Wake Forest, 5-1 in the ACC entering the weekend and their closest challenger. Win the conference and the automatic bid is waiting.
- **Lose only the ACC title game:** 11-2 and on the at-large bubble. Last year, the committee chair said Miami's head-to-head win was what moved the Hurricanes ahead of Notre Dame for the final at-large spot. That card now belongs to the Irish.
- **Lose a regular-season game and the ACC title game:** at 10-3, Miami is on the wrong side of the bubble. It can happen: last year Alabama became the first three-loss team to get an at-large bid, at 10-3, and the committee seeded it ahead of 10-2 Miami. But that Alabama team had an SEC schedule behind it, and the committee just watched Miami lose the one game on its schedule that looked like that.

Miami controls all of it. But a team that's 126th in FBS in pass-defense EPA doesn't have much margin for one more bad afternoon.

---

Ohio State was a few inches from a 21-0 lead and a few more from a 21-10 halftime lead, and lost by 13. Kentucky's record finally met a defense that could expose it, and the numbers were right all along. LSU's numbers were right too; it just couldn't cash them in. And Miami is still in control, but its pass defense is the reason it has to stay that way.
