# Stop calling him a weapon. Draft the running back.

---

Sports media has a workaround for rules it doesn't want to follow anymore.

When analysts are ready to mock Jeremiyah Love in the top 10, they don't call him a running back. They call him a "weapon." A "chess piece." A "matchup nightmare." An "offensive playmaker." Anything — literally anything — except the position he actually plays.

It's a tell.

Last week we looked at Caleb Downs and how the market punishes him for a label the data doesn't support. The "safety tax" discounts one of the best players in the class by 5-to-10 picks because of positional orthodoxy that falls apart the moment you check the numbers. Love is the same market, working the other direction. Instead of taxing the label, the draft-industrial complex just… swaps it. The rulebook says don't take running backs in the top 10. So we'll call him something else and take him in the top 10.

Here's the thing. The rulebook was already wrong.

## What the consensus is actually doing

The "don't draft running backs in round 1" orthodoxy has been gospel in analytics circles for most of the last decade. Surplus value curves showed RB as one of the weakest returns on premium draft capital. Teams that ignored the rule got hammered in the think-piece economy — Trent Richardson, Todd Gurley, Saquon Barkley at 2 were the case studies. Running backs "don't matter." Running backs "get hurt." Running backs "can be found in round 4."

Then Christian McCaffrey won Offensive Player of the Year. Then Bijan Robinson became an every-down centerpiece in Atlanta. Then Saquon — the guy the Giants "shouldn't" have taken at 2 — ran for 2,000 yards and won a Super Bowl.

The orthodoxy didn't die. It just got a new vocabulary.

Now the argument isn't "don't draft running backs early." It's "don't draft running backs early — *unless they're weapons*." Which is a completely different argument, and also not actually an argument. It's just a permission slip the media writes for itself when the tape is too good to ignore.

Love is the 2026 test case. A 6-foot, 206-pound back with 4.3 speed, genuine receiving chops, and the kind of highlight reel that makes GMs pick up the phone. He's in every top-15 mock this month. And every mock has the same tic — they spend two sentences on his usage profile before they land on the word "weapon," like they're trying to get it past a border guard.

So let's check the border guard's rulebook.

RB has a 22.6% boom rate, 22.6% bust rate, 0.0% differential. Those are neighbors, not different universes. The boom rates are within three points of each other. The bust rates are within two. If WR is premium and RB isn't, the data forgot to send the memo.

Now look at the Avg 4-Year AV column. Running backs lead every non-quarterback position on the board at 29.1. Higher than OL (26.3). Higher than LB (27.8). Higher than WR/TE (22.2). When first-round running backs hit, they hit the hardest of anyone on offense outside the quarterback room, because they touch the ball 250–300 times a year and the production piles up.

That is the opposite of what you've been told for a decade.

The surplus value argument isn't wrong about everything — round 2 and round 3 running backs do outperform the curve, and the "don't spend a first on a two-down back" version of the rule has real teeth. But at the top of round 1, for the guys who can do everything, the math flips. The boom rate is comparable to WR. The ceiling when they hit is higher. And the "weapons" the consensus is willing to draft early are mostly just… running backs with good PR.

## The Gibbs precedent

The reason the orthodoxy is wobbling is that the most recent test case buried it.

In 2023, the Detroit Lions traded *down* from pick 6 to pick 12 — picking up a second-rounder (Sam LaPorta) and a fifth in the deal — and used that 12th pick on Jahmyr Gibbs out of Alabama. A running back. At 12. After trading down.

The reaction was, predictably, that Brad Holmes had lost his mind. The analytics community called it the worst pick of the first round. "You don't draft a running back in the top 15." "You don't *trade down and still take a running back*." Mel Kiper gave the Lions a C+. PFF called the pick "curious." The surplus-value crowd treated it as a case study in what not to do.

Three years later, Gibbs is a two-time Pro Bowler. In 2024, he went for 1,412 rushing yards, 16 rushing touchdowns, 52 catches, 512 receiving yards, and 20 total scores — the fourth player in NFL history with a 1,900-scrimmage-yard, 16-rush-TD, 4-receiving-TD season, joining Marshall Faulk, David Johnson, and O.J. Simpson. In 2025, with David Montgomery traded and Gibbs the full-time RB1, he posted 1,839 scrimmage yards, 18 touchdowns, and a career-high 77 receptions for 616 yards. The Lions' offense runs through him. The LaPorta pick they got in the trade-down became a Pro Bowl tight end. The whole transaction is one of the best front-office sequences of the decade.

Every team that passed on that deal watched a franchise weapon come off the board at 12 and let Detroit take him.

Sound familiar? It should. Four years ago, 13 teams watched Kyle Hamilton fall to 14 because "you don't draft a safety that high." The Ravens picked him. He's now the highest-paid safety in NFL history and one of the 10 best defensive players in the league.

Different position, same market failure. Same root cause: positional orthodoxy overriding what the tape was obviously showing. Same result for the team that held its nose and drafted the player — franchise cornerstone at a discount created entirely by a label.

The Lions didn't need to call Gibbs a "weapon" to draft him at 12. They just drafted the running back. It has worked out fine.

## Love doesn't need the workaround

The second reason the "weapon" framing around Love is unnecessary is that his receiving profile doesn't require rhetorical cover. It holds up on its own.

In 2025 at Notre Dame, Love caught 27 passes for 280 yards and 3 touchdowns on 34 targets — a 79.4% catch rate averaging 10.4 yards per reception. Pro Football Focus graded him at 93.1 overall, second among 406 qualified FBS running backs in the country. Over his career he's caught 63 passes for 594 yards and 6 scores. He lines up in the slot. He runs actual routes. He's not a check-down safety valve being sold as a pass-catcher — he's a legitimate dual-threat back with receiving production that compares favorably to Gibbs at the same stage at Alabama.

So when an analyst says "Love is more than a running back, he's a weapon" — the first half is unnecessary and the second half is just a running back who catches passes. Which has been a position in football since 1960. Marshall Faulk wasn't a weapon. He was a running back. Roger Craig wasn't a chess piece. He was a running back. LaDainian Tomlinson didn't need a thesaurus.

The vocabulary inflation tells you everything about where the argument actually sits. If the data supported the "don't draft a RB at 8" rule, analysts wouldn't need the escape hatch. They need it because the data doesn't, and they know it.

## What we're not saying

Nobody here is arguing you should take a running back in the top 10 every year. The 2006–2020 sample has plenty of misses — Trent Richardson went 3rd overall and flamed out in two seasons. The "don't burn premium picks on two-down grinders" version of the rule is still correct. Volume-and-tackle-breaking backs without passing-game value get squeezed by modern NFL offenses, and that shows up in the tape every year.

The argument is specific: when the running back in question has dual-threat production, top-tier athleticism, and three years of tape against real competition, the boom-bust math is comparable to what you'd get drafting a wide receiver in the same range — and the ceiling is higher. Load-bearing dual-threat backs don't just match the WR hit rate, they out-produce it in raw value when they hit.

That is a defensible top-10 pick. It was defensible for Detroit in 2023. It's defensible for whoever takes Love in 2026. The "weapon" language is a workaround for a rule that the data stopped supporting years ago at the very top of round 1.

## The takeaway

Caleb Downs is getting discounted because of a label. Jeremiyah Love is getting relabeled to dodge a discount. Both stories are the same story — a draft market that makes decisions based on position names instead of players, and then retrofits the vocabulary to match what the tape is screaming at them.

The team that gets Love in the top 10 doesn't need to justify it by calling him something he isn't. They just need to be comfortable drafting a running back. The data says that's fine. The Lions' last three seasons say that's fine. The history of first-round RB hits says that's fine.

Stop calling him a weapon. Draft the running back.
