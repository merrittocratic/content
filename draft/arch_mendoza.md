# The Big Arch vs. The Mendoza Line

---

In baseball, the Mendoza Line is the .200 batting average — the bare minimum for a major leaguer to keep his job. Named after Mario Mendoza, it became shorthand for the floor of professional competence.

In the 2026 NFL Draft, there's a new Mendoza Line. Except this one isn't the floor. It's the ceiling. And the entire sports media establishment is pretending a quarterback who didn't reach it should have gone first anyway.

## The claim

Mike Greenberg, on ESPN's "Get Up," said it plainly: if Arch Manning were in this draft class, he'd be the number one overall pick over Fernando Mendoza. Dan Orlovsky went further on The Dan Patrick Show — "not even close," he said. Manning would be the "runaway number one pick. The pick would be made already."

Not even close.

A Heisman winner. A national champion. The FBS leader in passing touchdowns, passing efficiency, and QBR. A quarterback who went 16-0. And the argument is that a guy who went 10-3 with a 61% completion rate would leapfrog him, and it wouldn't even be competitive?

That's not analysis. That's last name recognition with a scouting report stapled to it.

## The table

Here's what actually happened in the 2025 college football season. Same year, same defenses, same sport.





Mendoza led the FBS in passing touchdowns, passing efficiency, and QBR. He won the Heisman in a landslide — first in all six geographic regions, the first player to do that since 2022. He won the Maxwell, the Davey O'Brien, the Walter Camp, AP Player of the Year, Big Ten Offensive Player of the Year. He led Indiana to its first national championship in school history, going 16-0 and beating Ohio State, Penn State, Alabama, Oregon, and Miami along the way.

Manning had a solid year. He improved down the stretch. He beat Michigan in the Citrus Bowl. But solid is not transcendent, and improving is not arrived.

The completion percentage gap alone should end this conversation. Mendoza completed 72% of his passes. Manning completed 61.4%. That's not a difference of opinion. That's ten percentage points of accuracy, which in the NFL translates directly to drive sustainability, third-down conversions, and wins. Orlovsky knows this. Greenberg knows this. They just don't care, because they're not making a data argument. They're making a vibes argument.

## We've seen this movie

If you've been reading Merrittocracy, you know the pattern. Since 2010, quarterbacks with 15 or fewer career Division I starts have a 75% bust rate when drafted in the first round. Trubisky, Haskins, Richardson — the graveyard is full.

Arch Manning has approximately 15 career starts. Twelve regular season games and a bowl game in 2025, plus two spot starts in 2024 when Quinn Ewers was injured. That's it. That's the résumé everyone wants to draft first overall.

The only quarterback to beat the 15-starts curse was Cam Newton, who was 6-6, 250 pounds, rushed for 1,473 yards, won the Heisman, won a national championship, and had a full season of competitive reps at Blinn College before his single year at Auburn. Newton wasn't an exception to the rule. He was a once-in-a-generation freak who barely survived it.

Manning rushed for 399 yards. Solid dual-threat ability. Not Newton. Not close to Newton. Not in the same area code as Newton.

## The Mannings already knew

Here's the part that should embarrass every analyst pushing this narrative.

In August 2025, before the season even started, Archie Manning told Texas Monthly that Arch wasn't going to declare for the 2026 draft. "Arch isn't going to do that. He'll be at Texas." Arch pushed back publicly — "I don't know where he got that from, he texted me to apologize" — and said he was taking it day by day.

Then he did exactly what Archie predicted.

In December, Cooper Manning texted ESPN to confirm: "Arch is playing football at Texas next year." Arch himself explained the decision: "I felt like I developed a lot this year, especially towards the back half, and I want to keep it going. There's no reason to leave."

The Mannings are not a normal football family. They are THE football family. Three generations of NFL quarterbacks. Archie played 11 seasons in the league. Peyton won two Super Bowls. Eli won two more. Between them, they've seen every version of this story play out — the early declares who weren't ready, the guys who went back and became franchise quarterbacks, and the ones who got talked into leaving too soon by a media machine that profits from the hype cycle regardless of what happens next.

Archie Manning didn't need a boom/bust model to know that 12 starts in the SEC wasn't enough. He's lived the data. When the patriarch of the most decorated quarterbacking family in NFL history tells you his grandson needs another year, maybe the correct response isn't "but the projection though."

## What they're actually saying

Strip away the TV production and the confident delivery, and the Manning-over-Mendoza argument reduces to three words: talent, projection, bloodline.

Talent is real. Manning has a big arm, he can move, and his late-season improvement was legitimate. Nobody is disputing the physical tools.

But projection is just a polite word for "the numbers don't support this yet." And bloodline is not a measurable. Peyton Manning was a four-year starter at Tennessee. Eli Manning was a three-year starter at Ole Miss. Neither of them entered the draft after one season of starting. The Manning family template is patience and development — the exact opposite of what Greenberg and Orlovsky are advocating.

Mendoza, meanwhile, isn't a projection. He's a result. Thirty-five career starts across Cal and Indiana. A 72% completion rate in a pro-style offense. Forty-one touchdowns against six interceptions. A national championship with the game's Most Valuable Offensive Player award. An NFL-ready passer who dissects coverages, processes quickly, and wins the biggest games on the biggest stages.

When the argument for one quarterback requires ignoring the stats and the argument for the other quarterback IS the stats, the choice isn't close. Orlovsky was right about that part. It's not even close. He just had the direction backwards.

## The Mendoza Line

In baseball, falling below the Mendoza Line means you probably shouldn't be in the lineup. In the 2026 NFL Draft, the Mendoza Line is what every other quarterback in this class is measured against — and nobody clears it.

Fernando Mendoza set the standard. Arch Manning's family had the good sense to recognize he wasn't there yet. The only people who can't see it are the ones paid to talk about it on television five days before the draft.

The Raiders will take Mendoza first on Wednesday. And sometime next April, Arch Manning — with another full season, another 12-plus starts, and a developmental arc that looks a lot more like his uncles' — will probably be in the conversation for real.

Patience isn't weakness. Ask a Manning.
