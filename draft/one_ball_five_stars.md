# One Ball, Five Stars

LeBron James is a Philadelphia 76er now. Two years, $8 million, a player option, and by his own description, "my last decision." Of the four teams seriously in the mix, it's the one that makes the least basketball sense, and that's not because the front office did anything wrong. They did almost everything right. That's the problem.

The fingerprints belong to Bob Myers. The same executive who built three championship rosters in Golden State now runs Philadelphia's basketball operation as President of Sports for Harris Blitzer, advising owner Josh Harris while Mike Gansey, poached off Cleveland's front office, handles the day to day. Their first big swing sent Paul George, two first-round picks, and two second-rounders to Boston for Jaylen Brown, the most shocking trade of the summer. Rich Paul, James's agent, later said the Brown deal "changed everything" for Philadelphia as a landing spot. That's not spin, that's the blueprint working as drawn up: trade for one Finals MVP, use him as the lure for a bigger one. Philadelphia went 45-37 last season and got swept in the second round by the eventual-champion Knicks. Ownership's answer wasn't a defensive upgrade. It was two more shot-creators.

Here's the part nobody trades for: touches. The Sixers now roll out Joel Embiid, Tyrese Maxey, Jaylen Brown, VJ Edgecombe, and James, four former All-Stars and a rookie already flashing star-level tools. Every one of them has, at some point, been the guy his team ran offense through. A basketball only needs one shooter at a time, and there are 24 seconds and one rim to split five ways. That math doesn't resolve itself just because the roster sheet looks like a Dream Team poster.

## Riley's Reunion, Canceled

Miami just gave up four rotation players and a stack of future first-round picks to land Giannis Antetokounmpo from Milwaukee. Pairing him with James would have closed a loop James opened over a decade ago, back when Pat Riley built him two championship runs in South Beach, this time with James as the wise co-star instead of the engine. The fit made more sense too. Miami needs shooting and secondary shot creation, not another isolation scorer, and a 41-year-old James playing a complementary role is close to ideal. The cap explanation is worth a second look, though. James signed for two years and $8 million, close to $4 million a year, nearly minimum-salary money for someone with his service time. Miami is hard-capped at the $209 million first apron after using an expanded trade exception on Giannis, but the Heat still had roughly $18 million of room under that line right after the trade closed. A $4 million deal fits inside that with space to spare. The more honest explanation is priorities, not room: Miami spent July locking up shooting depth around Giannis and Bam Adebayo rather than holding a roster spot open on a maybe, and a fifth ball-handler joining two bigs who both need touches in the paint might not have solved as much as it looked like on paper. Cap space was the story. Roster construction was probably the real one.

## The Warriors Tore Up Their Cap Sheet for Nothing

This one nearly happened. Draymond Green declined a $27.7 million player option specifically to clear the room Golden State needed to chase James in free agency and Anthony Davis in a trade, an attempt at building a Big Four around Stephen Curry before that era runs out of runway. A decade removed from their Finals wars, the rivalry-turned-reunion story told itself, and it was the only option offering real title contention this season instead of a two-year audition. Golden State did the work. James picked somewhere else anyway.

There's a consolation prize, though not a literal one. Rookie forward Yaxel Lendeborg, taken 11th out of Michigan, spent the summer saying LeBron is the player he studied growing up and personally lobbying him to sign, pitching the greatest shooter alive and a locker room full of ready-made mentors. Scouts comp him closer to Aaron Gordon or OG Anunoby than to James, so this isn't a stylistic clone. But he just carried Golden State to the Summer League championship game doing a credible seven-position impression of what the front office was chasing: size, defense, playmaking, a jumper. It doesn't erase missing on James. It does mean the Warriors' summer wasn't the disaster it looked like in late June.

## The Boring Answer Was the Right One

Cleveland required no math and no origin-story explainer. James already delivered the franchise its only championship there, and the current roster, Donovan Mitchell, James Harden, and Evan Mobley, made the Eastern Conference Finals last spring without him. He'd have walked into a team already built, needing an accelerant rather than a rescue. The shot-share tension is smaller there too. Mobley doesn't need the ball to matter. That's two ball-dominant guards and a two-way big, not four or five stars all reaching for the same 30 shots a night.

Philadelphia's front office built a roster that wins every press conference and highlight reel this fall. Whether it wins in April depends on something Myers and Gansey can't trade for: Embiid, Maxey, Brown, and James agreeing, possession by possession, on whose turn it is. Miami had the better sentimental fit. Golden State made the bigger sacrifice. Cleveland offered the cleanest math. Philadelphia offered the best press conference. Somebody's shot total takes a real hit this season, and it won't get decided in a war room.
