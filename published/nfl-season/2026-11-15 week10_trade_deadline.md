# Everything Must Go

The deadline gave us 17 in-season deals, and the teams that spent the most did not always buy the most. Jacksonville paid a 2027 first-round pick and two second-rounders. Chicago paid two firsts. San Francisco paid a fourth and a sixth for two starters. The price of a trade and the size of the hole it fills are two different numbers, and this year they barely correlate. Let's get into who patched a leak, who bought a name, and who is left holding the bag.

## Go Big Or Go Home

The Jaguars are 6-2 and went all in, sending a 2027 first, a 2027 second, a 2029 second and two later picks for defensive tackles Jordan Davis and Davon Godchaux and safety Jessie Bates III. The record is real, but the defense behind it is odd. Jacksonville is 3rd in EPA per play allowed (points added or lost per play versus an average play), yet 21st in yards per play allowed (5.22). The gap is takeaways. The Jags have 14 of them (3rd), along with a +8 turnover margin (2nd). Philadelphia and Indianapolis have the 29th and 30th offenses by EPA per play, and of the three top-10 offenses Jacksonville has faced, Cincinnati (2nd in EPA per play) scored 17 with Joe Burrow, New England (10th) scored 6 and Houston (8th) scored 34.

Here is the part that makes the trade make sense. In Weeks 1-6 the Jaguars allowed 72.7 rushing yards a game at 3.33 a carry. In Weeks 8-9, after DaVon Hamilton went down, that jumped to 141.5 and 4.88 per carry. Davis and Godchaux are the patch. Bates plugs the other hole: on throws 20-plus yards downfield, Jacksonville allows a 46% completion rate, 27th in EPA. Atlanta allowed 31% completions with Bates on 20-plus yard throws, 7th.

The problem is with the price tag. The first-rounder went for Davis, who plays between 35 and 65% of Philadelphia's defensive snaps. That is a lot to pay for a part-time nose tackle. The test is at Tennessee (1-7), where Jacksonville is a 10.5-point favorite. If Tony Pollard runs for 4.5 or more per carry, the hole still isn't patched yet.

## Pay No Attention To The Man Behind The Curtain

Everyone will talk about Keenan Allen, but the 49ers' real move was DeForest Buckner. San Francisco is 7-1 and 1st in EPA per play, success rate (the share of plays that keep the offense on schedule) and red-zone touchdown rate (85%). The 49ers have done this with Mike Evans out since Week 6 and Deebo Samuel out since Week 5, so a receiver was a reasonable shopping item.

Allen, though, is a chain-mover, not a difference-maker. With the Colts he averaged 43.9 yards a game and 6.05 yards per target, 59th of 66 receivers with 30-plus targets. His separation (distance to the nearest defender at the catch) ranks 101st of 118. He also has a 69% catch rate and 20 first downs. Brock Purdy is at 8.39 yards per attempt, and Allen's quarterback in Indianapolis, Daniel Jones, was at 5.71. A sixth-round price fits that.

Buckner for a fourth is the one that matters. The 49ers were already 1st in yards per carry allowed (3.43), but their sack rate is 5.2%, 27th. Buckner had 4 sacks and 8 QB hits in seven Colts games. He debuted in Week 9 on 68% of snaps with no sacks and 2 QB hits, and in his second game he faces Dallas, which takes sacks on only 4.0% of dropbacks (5th-best), in a game with the highest total on the board (55.5).

## Hello? Is Anybody Home?

Kansas City picked up Josh Downs for a fourth and a fifth. The Chiefs' receiver room behind Rashee Rice is thin: Xavier Worthy averages 5.91 yards per target, 60th of 66. Downs was at 6.21, 56th of 66, so the bet is that this number was more of a quarterback problem than a Downs problem. His separation (2.97 yards, 56th of 118) is mid-pack and well ahead of Allen's. Jones's completion rate above expectation was -2.0, 25th of 37, so a fourth and a fifth is a fair price for that bet.

The Colts are the ones who paid for it. At 2-7 they sold Allen, Downs, Buckner and Quenton Nelson for a third, two fourths, two fifths and a sixth. They were already last in the league in passing EPA, but Allen and Downs were 40% of their targets. In the two games since Nelson and Buckner left, offensive EPA per play went from -0.06 to -0.33 and rushing yards allowed from 126 a game to 182. The run defense was already poor, so the sale made a bad unit worse. It didn't break a good one. Jonathan Taylor, who stayed, ran for 69 yards on 21 carries with no touchdowns in those two games. Their opponent this week, Miami, has the league's 32nd-ranked pass defense by EPA per play, but against the run the Dolphins allow 4.15 a carry (14th). That makes this a softer test of Taylor than it looks. If Taylor can't run against the Dolphins, "stranded" is the right word.

## Two Out Of Three Ain't Bad

Two of the last three deals look like wins. Minnesota getting Kirk Cousins for a third is the biggest upgrade in the market. The Vikings' quarterbacks were Kyler Murray at -0.06 EPA per dropback (hamstring, not practicing) and Carson Wentz at -0.27 EPA per dropback. Cousins in Las Vegas was at +0.15, and he was sacked on just 3.3% of dropbacks. Minnesota's offensive line allows a 9.2% sack rate, 27th, so protection could be an issue for a 38-year-old quarterback. Jeffery Simmons went to the Bears for two firsts and a fourth. He's already had 2 sacks and 3 tackles for loss in two games in Chicago. The Bears are 7-2 with a -4 turnover margin, tied 25th, on 17 giveaways and 13 takeaways, and ten of those 17 giveaways came in two games. The record is ahead of the profile again, but Simmons is making the defense better, and that is what the Bears paid for.

The miss is Houston, which spent a second-round pick on Marvin Harrison Jr. In three games he has 11 targets, 6 catches, 60 yards and 1 touchdown, on 19%, 21% and 46% of snaps. The passing game was not the problem: Stroud's completion rate above expectation is +5.9 (3rd of 37) and Houston has the fewest giveaways in the league with 4. What is wrong with the Texans is that they are 1-4 in one-score games, average 3.67 a carry (30th) and rank 18th in defensive EPA per play. A receiver does not fix any of that.
