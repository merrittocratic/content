# We're Not In Kansas Anymore

Viewer Discretion Advised!  This is a long one, so if you just don't feel like reading amazing content tonight that's OK.  Just make sure to stop by the love button on you're way out.

Every week I flip a switch in my content folder from college football to the NFL and back again. It's literally one line in one file, and lately I'm not sure it matters. College players change teams every offseason like free agents. The 12-team playoff, with a 24-team version on the table, hands automatic bids to conference champions, the same way the NFL is about to hand one to whoever *"wins"* the NFC South. And the AP poll and the playoff committee keep ranking records, while the numbers say they are lying. This is exactly what we covered last week with the Vikings. The record said 6-0, but everything else pointed to a good team, not a 6-0 one. Go figure, they lost this week.  So I went looking for whether college football is actually turning into the NFL, or just wearing its jersey.

## You Get A 4-Star! You Get A 4-Star!

The NFL's parity comes through the draft and the salary cap spreading good players around. College football doesn't have these structural limits in place, but it's starting to get a similar result anyway.

247Sports' talent composite scores every roster by the recruiting ratings of its players, transfers included. In 2022, the five most talented rosters averaged 161 points more than the teams ranked 6th through 25th in the composite. By 2025 that gap was down to 119, the smallest it's ever been, and it's only 127 this year. The top didn't get worse. The middle class got better: the top five used to rate 1.45 times the teams ranked 26th to 50th in talent, and now it's down to 1.33 times. You can see it at the top of the College Football Playoff rankings, where three of the committee's top 10 don't even have a top-35 roster by talent: Utah is 3rd in the CFP rankings and 58th in the talent composite, Indiana is 4th and 38th, and BYU is 9th and 59th.

This is also being reflected in the betting markets. The point spread is how many points the market expects the favorite to win by, and in games between two FBS teams the typical spread was right around 10 points from 2014 to 2019. Since 2023, this number has been coming down and is now hovering between 7.5 and 8.5. The NFL's typical spread is 3.5, so while college isn't there yet, it's heading that way. Some of this may be conference realignment, but this Saturday perfectly encapsulates what these spreads are starting to look like.  The committee's #1, Miami, is a 4.5-point underdog at #11 Notre Dame, and #2 Georgia is laying just 3 at #10 Ole Miss.

So if the matchups are getting closer, does that mean more upsets? Not the way you'd think. At the same spread, college underdogs already win more often than NFL underdogs. From 2014 to 2025, teams getting 7 to 13.5 points won 25.8% of the time in college and 21.8% in the NFL. "Any given Sunday" doesn't mean bad NFL teams beat good ones more often. It means NFL games start close. NFL favorites win about 64% of the time, college favorites about 76%. College will get more chaotic as the spreads keep shrinking due to the increase in talent in the football middle class, not because 10-point underdogs suddenly start winning more frequently.  Well, unless you're the #2 team in the country this year, then you're in trouble.

## Show Me The Money!

If the talent is being spread around, the next question is how it's moving. The NFL has free agency. College has the transfer portal, and they're starting to look like the same thing. The portal numbers exploded by almost 3x from 1,770 in 2021 to 4,484 this year.  In 2025, a 105-man roster limit was put in place, which seems to be stalling portal growth as last year's numbers were similar at 4,499. 

What makes the portal a market and not an all-out fire sale is that players actually find buyers. About 4 in 5 entrants now land at a new school, up from 3 in 5 in 2021, and the median number of transfers that schools are taking went from 3 to 11. There are always outliers though.  Oklahoma State took 55 this year. Fifty-five! That's not a recruiting class, that's an expansion draft.

Like the NFL, there's more than one way to build a contender. UCLA is 8-0 and #5 in the AP poll with 43 incoming transfers this cycle, the most of anyone in the AP top 7. The blue bloods shop differently. Georgia took 9 transfers and Notre Dame took 7, both in the bottom 6% of the 69 power-conference teams (Notre Dame included) by count. But the Bulldogs, the Irish and Ohio State all rank in the top 20% for four- and five-star transfers. The Bruins are filling out the roster in bulk. Georgia only shops on the top shelf.

Large wholesale roster changes are a quick way to get the right players in the building and set a new culture of winning quickly.  Indiana did it in 2024 before they won it all last year, and UCLA is doing it this year.  I'm skeptical UCLA is a true title contender, but they've righted the ship and next year with a more targeted portal class they could make a Hoosier-like run to a Natty.

The portal isn't the reason the top teams keep losing. The six teams that have beaten a #1 or #2 this year took a median of 20 transfers, which is right on target for power-conference teams overall. It changed how teams get built. It didn't make the top teams beatable.

## You Sit On A Throne Of Lies!

Last week I said if the Vikings couldn't score on Detroit's defense, their 6-0 record had a ceiling. Well, we found it. Minnesota lost 19-15 in Detroit on Sunday, the first time all year it faced a team the market rated as roughly its equal (the Vikings were 3-point underdogs). The defense is still 1st in EPA per play allowed, the offense is still 29th in EPA per play, and five of the six wins came by 10 points or fewer against opponents with a combined 17-25 record. That doesn't make the Vikings a fraud. They're still about 6th in net EPA per play, which is a good team. Just not the team 6-0 said they were.

College football has that problem every single week, the AP poll mostly ranks records exactly the way NFL standings do, but computer ranking systems actually rate the quality of a team.  To compare the two, I lined the polls up against strength of record, which grades how impressive a record is given who a team played, and a computer consensus: each team's median rank across six measures (point differential, SRS, Elo, FPI, SP+ and EPA per play) out of 138 FBS teams.

You'd hope the playoff committee, with every one of those computers in the room, would split the difference. Its first rankings came out Tuesday night, and every team in this table except New Mexico landed within five spots of its AP rank.



Kentucky is the Minnesota Vikings of college football. The Wildcats are 7-1 and 8th in the AP poll, and the committee moved them up to 6th in the CFP rankings. Their record is the 2nd most impressive in the country by strength of record. The problem: every efficiency measure ranks them somewhere between 14th and 49th, and their median computer rank is 36.5. Even unbeaten UCLA, 8th in the CFP rankings, only has a median computer rank of 15. New Mexico shows why the schedule matters: the Lobos' point differential is 5th in the country, but adjusted for who they played they're 45th in SRS (margin of victory adjusted for opponents). That's the one place the committee broke from the poll: New Mexico is 22nd in the AP poll and left out of the CFP top 25 entirely.

Then there's the mirror image. LSU is 6-2 and 14th in the CFP rankings, but 1st in SRS and 1st in EPA per play, with a median computer rank of 3.5. Ohio State is 6-2 and 12th in the CFP rankings, but 1st in SP+ and 2nd in FPI, with a median computer rank of 6. SP+ and FPI still carry some preseason weighting this time of year, so you could argue the Buckeyes are getting a little credit for being Ohio State. LSU doesn't have that excuse. SRS and EPA per play are built entirely from this season's games, and the Tigers lead both. If I'm picking an opponent in December, I'm taking 7-1 Kentucky over 6-2 LSU or Ohio State, and it isn't close.

## If You Ain't First, You're Last

Ricky Bobby never played in the NFC South. The whole division has a losing record: Carolina leads at 3-4, Tampa Bay and Atlanta are 3-5 and New Orleans is 2-5. It's the only division in the league without a team over .500, and one of them is getting a home playoff game.

If you're picking one, pick a team that's currently tied for second. Tampa Bay is 3-5 with a +27 point differential and ranks 10th in net EPA per play, behind the league's 2nd-best defense by EPA per play allowed. The other three range from 17th (Carolina) to 27th (New Orleans) in net EPA. The Bucs are the only team in the division playing like a playoff team.

The NFC South has been here before. Since the 2002 realignment, seven division winners have finished .500 or worse, and three came from the NFC South, including last year's 8-9 Panthers. Those teams didn't just show up and lose, though. Four of the seven won their home wild-card game, including 7-9 Seattle over the defending champion Saints in 2010 and Tim Tebow's 8-8 Broncos over Pittsburgh in 2011. All four lost the next week, but a bad NFL division winner is still an NFL team hosting another NFL team.

College football copied the structure. The 12-team playoff guarantees spots to the highest-ranked conference champions, and in both 12-team fields so far, the lowest-rated teams by season's end were champions with automatic bids:

- **2024:** Boise State (12-1, 30th in SRS, 29th in SP+), Arizona State (11-2, 16th in SRS, 35th in SP+) and Clemson (10-3, 23rd in SRS, 22nd in SP+).
- **2025:** Tulane (11-2, 56th in SRS, 45th in SP+) and James Madison (12-1, 29th in SRS, 27th in SP+).

Those five went 0-5 and lost by about 17 points a game, from Texas' 39-31 win over Arizona State to Ole Miss' 41-10 win over Tulane. Weak NFL division winners won four of their seven home playoff games. Weak college champions haven't won one. College took the NFL's automatic bids without the NFL's parity at the top.

This year's leading candidates are spread across three leagues. Boise State (7-1, 25th in the CFP rankings, median computer rank of 32), now in the rebuilt Pac-12, is the only Group of Six team the committee ranked and would hold the automatic bid today. New Mexico (7-1, 22nd in the AP poll but unranked by the committee, median computer rank of 36.5) is the Mountain West's best hope, and in the Sun Belt, James Madison is unranked but has a median computer rank of 25. Whoever takes that bid is my pick to be this year's one-and-done. Whoever comes out of the NFC South, especially if it's Tampa Bay, has a better shot at a playoff win than any of them.

## You Must Choose. But Choose Wisely.

If college football is going to keep borrowing from the NFL, and a 24-team playoff would be the biggest loan yet, it should borrow the part that makes Sundays worth watching: games between teams that are actually close. I'm not asking anyone to give up the cupcakes. Play your two Group of Five or FCS games, sell the tickets, rest the starters. I just want the one power-conference, non-conference game to mean something.

Here's my proposal post-24 team playoff introduction. Every power-conference team plays one non-conference power opponent a year, and that opponent has to come from a pool of the 12 teams from outside its own conference that are closest to it in the talent composite, averaged over the last five years. Over any five-year stretch, you pick five different opponents from your pool. Rivalry games like Iowa-Iowa State and Florida-Florida State stay on the schedule, on top of the pool game, not instead of it.

Ohio State's pool in a given year would be Georgia, Alabama, Texas, Texas A&M, Clemson, LSU, Notre Dame, Oklahoma, Florida, Miami, Tennessee and Auburn. Kentucky's would include Wisconsin, UCLA, Washington, TCU and Nebraska, so the committee would finally see the Wildcats against a peer from another league before deciding what 7-1 is worth. And Indiana, last year's champion, is one of nine power-conference teams that didn't schedule a single non-conference power opponent this year.  A big reason why there is alot of skepticism about the Hoosiers outside of Bloomington.

There's some evidence it would work. Since 2021, leaving out Notre Dame, favorites have won 61% of power-conference non-conference games between teams that would share a pool, and 73% of the ones outside it. That 61% sits right next to the NFL's 64%. Notre Dame shows what the alternative looks like. As an independent, every game the Irish play is a non-conference game, and this year Stanford and Michigan State spent their only power-conference non-conference game as 40- and 29-point underdogs in South Bend.

---

College football has the NFL's skeleton now: a free-agent market, automatic bids for weak champions and a poll that grades the standings. What it doesn't have yet is the NFL's parity. The middle class is closing the gap and the spreads are shrinking with it, but the weak champions are still getting run off the field in December. We're not in Kansas anymore. We're just not in the NFL yet either.
