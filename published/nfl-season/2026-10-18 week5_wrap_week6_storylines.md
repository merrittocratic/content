# It's Not You, It's Me

Every breakup comes with the same speech. Arizona just gave one to Marvin Harrison Jr., shipping him to Houston. C.J. Stroud and Dak Prescott could give it to their teams' records. And Jayden Daniels gave a version of it himself after Washington ran out of time in overtime: "ultimately, though, it's on me." The question in all three: who's actually to blame?

## Show Me The Targets

Harrison wasn't benched in Arizona. He was ignored. He played 74% of the offense's snaps this year, but drew just 13 targets in four games, an 8.3% target share (his slice of the team's targets), down from 22.6% as a rookie. That was a choice by new head coach Mike LaFleur, not a sign Harrison couldn't get on the field.

The fair knock is separation. As a rookie, Harrison averaged 2.05 yards between himself and the nearest defender when the ball arrived, 95th of 96 qualifying receivers. But he also got bad balls. FTN's charting graded 68% of his targets as catchable in 2024 and 65% in 2025, against a league rate of about 74%. When he did get good looks in 2025, he ranked 13th of 78 receivers in EPA per target (expected points added, how many points a play adds compared with an average play in that situation). That's not a bust.

Harrison's exit spawned a theory: with him gone, defenses will double Trey McBride. Nobody publishes double coverage, but FTN charts contested targets, where a defender was in position to contest the catch. Arizona's contested rate jumped from 18.9% to 30.4% in Week 5. The catch: the opponent was Detroit, which forces contested catches more than all but two defenses. And a receiver who drew 13 targets in four games wasn't pulling a safety away from anyone. The theory doesn't hold.

Back in September I said Jeremiyah Love could turn out to be Saquon Barkley or Clyde Edwards-Helaire, and nothing before Week 1 could tell you which. Five games in, he's closer to the second. Like Harrison, he's sharing the field, under half the snaps in four of five games. But this one isn't all usage. Love averages 3.66 yards per carry (24th of 35 backs) and is 0.39 yards per carry below expected (29th of 42), a stat that already accounts for his blockers. He isn't facing stacked boxes either. Of the eight top-10 backs drafted since 2015, his first five games rank last in scrimmage yards per game (56.2) and yards per carry.

Sunday at the Rams, the market has Arizona at about 19.5 points. Our model gives Love a 25% chance of a startable week (RB24, against an Expert Consensus Ranking of RB19). Harrison is set to debut for Houston at WR77 on our board, 25 spots below consensus.

## Close, But No Cigar

Houston is 1-4, and Stroud is 3rd in the league in completion percentage over expected (CPOE, accuracy adjusted for how hard each throw is). In Week 3, I said Stroud wasn't Houston's problem. The record agrees: the Texans have scored 110 points and allowed 112, which normally produces about 2.5 wins, and they have one. That's the biggest shortfall in the league. They've lost three one-score games and won none.

But Stroud owns part of it. His EPA per play falls from 0.164 in the first three quarters to 0.031 in the fourth quarter and overtime, and Houston is 22nd in red-zone touchdown rate.

Dallas is the cleaner case. Prescott is 7th in EPA per play and at his best late, 0.447 per play in the fourth quarter and overtime. The offense is 4th in third-down rate and 3rd in red-zone touchdown rate. In that same Week 3 piece I called Dallas the exact opposite of Houston, and it still is: the defense is dead last in EPA per play, and the Cowboys are 2-3 with three one-score losses of their own.

Sunday in London, Jacksonville's run defense is 1st in yards per carry allowed, but its pass defense is 23rd in yards per attempt allowed. The game is on Stroud's arm. Our model gives him a 24% chance of a startable week (QB19, against a consensus QB15).

## Hurry Up And Wait

Daniels' efficiency came back this year: 0.137 EPA per play, up from -0.018 in 2025. His accuracy didn't. His CPOE is -0.2, 24th of 30 quarterbacks, and his success rate (the share of plays that keep an offense on schedule) has fallen three straight years, to 42.9%. He's running less, 1.3 designed runs a game, down from 3.9 as a rookie, and holding the ball longer, 2.97 seconds before each throw (10th-longest of 37). Before Week 1, I asked whether we'd get the rookie star or last year's brittle rollercoaster. So far it's neither: it's a quarterback Washington is trying to protect.

Which brings us to the overtime clock against the Giants. Tied 27-27, no Washington timeouts. At 0:55, Rachaad White ran up the middle for 2 yards, in bounds, to the Giants' 24. The next snap came at 0:12. That's 43 seconds between snaps, the longest of 63 comparable situations since 2024, where the median was 16. Daniels was sacked on that snap, and time expired. The sack got the headlines, but the game was lost on the in-bounds run and the 43 seconds that followed. Daniels owned the operation. The play call, an in-bounds run with no timeouts, deserves its share too.

Monday night at undefeated San Francisco, the 49ers allow 6.25 yards per pass attempt, 2nd-fewest in the league. A quarterback taking 2.97 seconds to throw is the pressure point. Our model is more optimistic than the market, with a 45% chance of a startable week (QB5, against a consensus QB7).

---

Harrison was ignored more than he failed, and Love's problem is more Love than the line. Stroud's record is unlucky, but not only unlucky. And Daniels lost the clock a play before he lost the snap.
