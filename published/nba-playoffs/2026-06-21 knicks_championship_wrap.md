# It Took 12 Points to Erase 53 Years

---

San Antonio led by double digits in every game of this series. They outscored the Knicks in the first quarter by 57 points across five games.  While there is some variation, most of the data cites the Spurs leading for 177 of the 240 total minutes played in the series (roughly 74%).  

They still lost 4-1.

We can hold off on any Wembanyama ceiling takes right now. The 2026 NBA Finals were decided by fourth-quarter personnel math, and San Antonio ran out of it almost very time it mattered. Understanding how a team leads Q1 of five consecutive Finals games and still loses the series is the only honest accounting of what just happened.

## Unfathomamable, Unfath..., It's without Fathoms

The New York Knicks won the NBA championship by a net margin of just 12 points across five games:  10, 1, minus-4, 1, and 4. That's just a net point differential per game of +2.4.  That's the lowest point differential per game in recent memory for a team with a 3-1 edge going into Game 5.  



Every other 3-1 lead in a Finals series in the last twelve years was held by a team winning games by at least six points on average. The only team to blow a 3-1 Finals lead in that span, the 2016 Warriors, was winning by 7.3 points per game through four. The Knicks were barely above 2.0, with two of their three wins being decided by a single basket, including the largest single-game comeback win in NBA Finals history.

## Put. That Coffee. Down. Coffee's for closers.

[TABLE IMAGE: q1_q4_margins.png]

San Antonio won every first quarter. They outscored the Knicks by 57 combined Q1 points across five games, built double-digit leads in every game, and came out ready every single night.

Then the fourth quarter arrived.

The Knicks outscored the Spurs by 26 points in Q4 across the series, winning the fourth quarter in three of five games. In Game 2, they'd already built enough cushion that an eight-point Spurs comeback came up short. The formula we outlined before the series was right, just not in the direction the Spurs needed. When Castle operates as a defender-first, the team functions. When he has to score and stop Brunson in the same fourth quarter, something gives. In Game 5 it was both at once: Castle went 1-for-10, KAT fouled out with two points and five turnovers, and the Spurs got outscored 29-18 in the fourth despite a seven-point lead entering it.

That's a closing problem. And the man who was supposed to solve it was De'Aaron Fox.

Back in April, when this playoff run started, we laid out the Kareem precedent and drew the obvious conclusion: Fox is good, but Fox is not Oscar Robertson, and Fox is definitely not Magic Johnson. The distance between Fox and the right co-star was most of what separated "Wemby could win this" from "Wemby wins this." In May, during the Conference Finals with Fox sidelined, we asked whether his return would settle the Robin problem or just add a third candidate to a rotation that couldn't decide. Batman was locked in, we wrote, but the yellow cape was still unclaimed.

The yellow cape is still unclaimed.

Fox is San Antonio's highest-paid player. He averaged 19 points per game in the regular season and signed a four-year, $229 million extension. In the Finals he averaged 15.9 points on 42.8% shooting and never once led the Spurs in scoring across the entire postseason. In Game 4, with the Spurs holding a one-point lead and needing only to run out the clock, Fox held the ball in a runout and then attempted a layup. Anunoby blocked it. The Knicks got the ball back, Brunson missed a three, and Anunoby tipped it in with 1.2 seconds left. The Spurs' largest lead in Finals history had become a one-point loss, and Fox's decision to shoot rather than dribble it out is what opened the door. Three quarters later in the closing minutes of an elimination game, he missed two open three-point attempts when the Spurs needed a bucket.

Coffee's for closers. Fox is not a closer.

The Harper side of the equation is more complicated. His Game 5 box score: 25 points, 53% shooting, five rebounds, four assists, zero turnovers, looked like the answer. His plus-minus, -12 in 31 minutes, asked a harder question. Castle went 1-for-10 with 6 points and finished -1. Single-game plus-minus in a 4-point game is noisy, and lineup timing likely explains part of the gap. But a player who scores 25 and finishes -12 is giving something back somewhere, and the pattern rhymes with something that predates this series.

Harper is 20 years old. He became the fourth rookie in NBA history to score 20 or more points in consecutive Finals games, joining Alvan Adams (1976), Tom Heinsohn (1957), and Joe Fulks (1947). The youngest player ever to score 20-plus in a Finals game. He set the Spurs franchise record for rookie points in a single postseason, passing David Robinson's 243 from 1990.

His Finals line: 16, 15, 13, 21, 25. The trajectory matters as much as the average.

[TABLE IMAGE: harper_finals_line.png]

Last season at Rutgers, Harper and Ace Bailey, another projected lottery pick, played together. Rutgers went 15-17. Two elite individual offensive talents, a losing record. That is not a verdict on Harper's ceiling. It is a data point worth sitting with when the question is whether spectacular offensive production and winning consistently travel together in his game.

The series data rhymes with it. His worst shooting game was Game 3, the only Spurs win. His two best games were losses. The box score measures what Harper does when he scores. The plus-minus keeps asking what happens to the team while he's doing it.

His father Ron Harper won five championships as a role player for Jordan's Bulls and Shaq's early Lakers. Dylan has already produced one of the more historically significant rookie Finals performances in fifty years. Whether he becomes the winning player his talent implies is the question this series raised more than it answered. 

## The Sum is Greater than its Parts

The contrast between the two teams is stark.  Wemby, Castle, and Harper are all young Spurs' cornerstones from recent draft lotteries.  The Knicks have zero homegrown contributing lottery picks.

Jalen Brunson came from Dallas in free agency. OG Anunoby came from Toronto in a trade. Josh Hart came from Portland in a trade. Mikal Bridges came from Brooklyn in a trade. Karl-Anthony Towns came from Minnesota in a trade. The team that just ended 53 years of disappointment at the Mecca didn't bottom out for a supermax pick. They had a star in Brunson who was willing to take less money and the were better at acquiring good players than everyone else in the room.  In the **process** they built a culture that could erase a 29-point deficit and also win 3 road playoff games to bring home the title.

There is no glamorous thesis here. No draft lottery moment, no singular night when the franchise turned. Just sustained front-office execution across years, reading the market, moving assets at the right time, assembling a roster greater than the sum of its parts. Every front office that bottomed out chasing a generational pick can now point to this roster and ask whether the plan was actually optimal. Some of them took the right pick and still waited a decade.

Wembanyama is 22. When Harper is ready to be the co-star every night, and *maybe* he's close, this team becomes a different problem for whoever they play in June. The Spurs will be back. Whether they can win it all will not be because of Wemby's ceiling, but most likely because of someone else's

The Knicks earned this. Every close win. Every tipped ball. 

Fifty-three years needed just 12 points in the right *New York Minutes* to end the drought.  That's the most Knick way it could have gone.
