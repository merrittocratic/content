# The Tax Man Cometh

After a mid-season acquistion, Cleveland wasn't supposed to be worried about surviving Jurassic Park in Toronto.

The Cavaliers entered the playoffs with Donovan Mitchell, Evan Mobley, and James Harden, a lineup assembled through a combination of draft capital, player development, and a February deadline swing for a future Hall of Famer. The Raptors entered as the five-seed in the middle of a rebuild that isn't quite finished yet.  Somehow this series is way more competitive than expected.

That gap between expectation and reality is what the superteam tax looks like when the bill arrives.

## What did it Cost?  Everything.

OK, settle down Thanos.  In 2022, Cleveland traded a haul of players and first-round picks to Utah to acquire Donovan Mitchell. Then in February, they traded Darius Garland, a young All-Star-caliber guard, and a second-round pick to the Clippers for Harden.

The logic was somewhat defensible. Harden was playing well in Los Angeles. Garland was good, but injury prone. The move was designed to go *deeper* in the playoffs, not to survive a first-round series against a team rebuilding around Scottie Barnes.  Admittedly, I watched Barnes play at FSU, and I really didn't get it, but he has blossomed into a force this year.  Don't tell my wife, but I am wrong sometimes.

But here's what superteam math almost always misses: you're not just buying a player. You're trading future flexibility for present upside.  That present upside often results in your championship aspirations turning into dust.

## I did not see that coming...

Here is where the Harden logic being *defensible* falls apart, and falls apart fast. No denying, James Harden is one of the greatest offensive players in NBA history. But when you're making an acquistion to go **deeper** in the playoffs, maybe you don't choose a guy who has one of the most well-document performance drags between the playoffs and the regular season in the NBA.

The chart above isn't just indicting Harden the player, it's indicting the premise that what you're getting in February is the same as what you're getting in May. Toronto figured out quickly that trapping Harden and Mitchell simultaneously and daring the rest of the roster to beat them was a viable strategy, because the rest of the roster is exactly what Cleveland traded away to get Harden in the first place.

That's the circular logic at the center of the superteam tax. You acquire a star to compensate for roster depth. Acquiring the star depletes roster depth. The depth you traded away is precisely what the other team exploits in the playoffs.

OK got it, so don't get the player who wore a fat suit?

## Two teams, same bill

It's not quite that simple.  If Cleveland is the slow-motion version of this story, Phoenix is what it looks like at full speed. The Suns traded a massive haul for Kevin Durant and Bradley Beal.  Young players.  Multiple first-round picks.  The kind of assets that build contenders over time . The Goal was a championship. The result: both players are no longer on the roster, the Suns were an eight-seed, and they just got swept by Oklahoma City.

OKC, for the record, built their roster with assets. Draft picks. Patient development. The exact kind of moves that Phoenix gave away. The second best team in the West, San Antonio, didn't trade for their cornerstone, they waited for him. No assets surrendered, no future mortgaged, no elaborate sign-and-trades. The Spurs did the unglamorous thing: they were bad for a couple of years, got the right lottery ball, and let a generational talent develop, while putting pieces around him that made sense. It's the direct opposite of what Cleveland and Phoenix tried to do. 

OKC and the Spurs look like the class of the West. Both built through the draft. Neither mortgaged the future to rent a ceiling. The bracket this year is practically a controlled experiment: on one side you have teams that paid the superteam tax; on the other, you have the teams that collected on it.

## Even a broken clock...

Superteam construction isn't always wrong. Miami's Big Three worked. Golden State is complicated but their dynasty was draft-built first and augmented second. The approach can work, it just works far less often than the teams attempting it seem to believe.  The failures are often brutal because the teams that try it are left without the assets to rebuild.  Hello Milwaukee!

Cleveland's situation isn't hopeless. They can still win this series. But the fact that it's even a series, the fact that a front office that spent this much is watching a 2-2 scoreboard against Toronto, is the data point. The gap between what was purchased and what's been delivered is exactly the gap the superteam tax predicts.

While you can buy a ceiling, you can't buy certainty. And in the first round, against a team nobody was worried about two weeks ago, that distinction matters.
