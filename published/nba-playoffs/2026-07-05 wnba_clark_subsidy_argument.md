# When Everyone's Super, No One Turns a Profit

---

America turns 250 today! Two and a half centuries of betting that letting the market decide who wins beats letting a committee decide who deserves to. Greatest run in the history of the world, not up for debate!  

The problem: there's a professional sports league operating in this country right now that has spent two years demonstrating what a business looks like when it doesn't have to make that bet. It's the WNBA. I know, I know I said I would never write about this league, but the power of truth compels me.  Plus, a little dose of why Capitalism beats Socialism is good way to celebrate the birth of the Greatest Nation in history.  

## That Kind of Sounds a bit like Socialism

Let's clear the underbrush before somebody does it for us in the replies. Nobody's suggesting Cathy Engelbert nationalized women's basketball. The teams are privately owned. Players get a paycheck, not a ration card. "Socialist" isn't a legal classification here, it's a description of an incentive structure: a business that gets to prioritize the equal standing of the collective over the growth of its most valuable individual, because somebody else is covering the difference when that choice costs money. Strip out the literal definition and just watch what the league actually does with its most bankable player, and the shoe fits uncomfortably well.

## The Warmth of Collectivism

Here's the plain math. Adam Silver has said, on the record, more than once, that there's no short-term plan to make this league profitable, and that's fine by him. The WNBA lost somewhere in the neighborhood of $40 to $50 million last full season by its own accounting. The NBA owns roughly 60% of the league and has been covering the gap, reportedly to the tune of something like $15 million a year, since 1996. Silver, on why: "we're not selling enough tickets to run a viable business."

Now the other half of the math. A finance professor at Indiana University ran the numbers on the 2024 season and concluded Caitlin Clark alone accounted for more than a quarter of the WNBA's total revenue that year:  attendance, television, merchandise, all of it. The Fever's average home crowd went from about 4,000 fans a night to over 17,000 the moment she showed up. Road games got moved to bigger arenas because the originals couldn't hold the demand. Six different television networks posted their most-watched WNBA broadcast ever during her rookie season. An 11-year, $2.2 billion media rights deal landed this year, and it doesn't land without her rookie and sophomore seasons proving the audience was real.

A business that needs $40 million a year from a parent company to keep the lights on, and has one player statistically responsible for a quarter of its revenue, normally protects that player like the building's on fire. A business that **doesn't** have to worry about the light bill can afford to treat her as one asset among many, interchangeable with whichever rookie breaks out next, because the check clears either way.

## Syndrome Wanted Socialism Too!

There's a scene late in *The Incredibles* where the villain finally explains the actual plan, and it isn't the plan you'd expect from a supervillain. He's not trying to rule the world. He's trying to hand out powers to everyone, so nobody's special anymore. "When everyone's super," he says, "no one will be." It's the most quietly radical line in a kids' movie, and it is, almost word for word, the argument a Emanuel Acho made about Caitlin Clark on a podcast three days ago.

He didn't dress it up. His case: Clark did her job, the job was dragging the league's attention over some invisible threshold, the threshold has been crossed, and the WNBA has plenty of other talent to lean on now. His conclusion, verbatim: "the WNBA could and would be better off without Caitlin Clark."

Set aside whether he's right about the basketball for a second and just notice the shape of the argument. It isn't "she's overrated." It's "she did her job, the job was raising the floor for everyone else, and now that the floor's raised, the specific person who raised it is expendable." Nobody makes that argument about a Fortune 500 company's most productive salesperson. You make that argument about a member of a commune who finished building the barn.

It also happens to describe, with unsettling precision, how the league's own officiating has behaved. Clark has absorbed nine flagrant fouls since entering the league in 2024, the most recent a closed fist to the throat from Alyssa Thomas that officials didn't call live and only upgraded to a Flagrant 2 and a one-game suspension after the replay went viral. Clark's coach's real complaint afterward wasn't the missed call. It was the pattern: "she is not called the same way everybody else is called." A league that needed its highest earner on the floor the way a struggling business needs its best salesperson in the room doesn't wait for a news cycle to enforce rules that already exist. It doesn't have to. It can absorb the cost of looking away, because somebody else is covering the loss regardless.

## The Fever Without Her

The honest counter-argument deserves its own paragraph, because it's a good one. The same Indiana Fever that's setting attendance records now was, one year before Clark arrived, one of the worst teams in the league -- with Kelsey Mitchell already on the roster and Aliyah Boston fresh off winning Rookie of the Year. Two legitimately good players, and the roster was still bad enough to land the No. 1 pick. Compare that to 1996: Magic, Bird, Barkley, Stockton, and Malone were already walking Hall of Fame plaques, and the NBA still needed Michael Jordan specifically to become a global business. A good roster and a good business are not the same category. The NBA figured that out decades before the WNBA had to.

And to be fair, the league did eventually market Jordan like the asset he was:  the Nike deal, the world tours, the whole apparatus built around one guy. Nobody's saying the WNBA hasn't tried a version of this with Clark; the bigger arenas and the magazine covers are real. But marketing a star in the highlight package and protecting her during the actual game are two different commitments, and the league keeps proving it will fund the first one enthusiastically and the second one only under public pressure.

## Cherry Picking Stats

"There are liars, damn liars, and there are statistics".  During last season when Caitlin Clark missed over half the season and the playoffs with injury, there was a narrative going around that was effectively: "the viewership was up even without her".  It doesn't survive a closer look. That number came from a Nielsen tally through the season's first 56 nationally televised games, reported in late July ,a stretch still loaded with her early-season appearances before the injuries piled up. Measured cleanly, the picture flips hard: during a five-game stretch that summer when Clark was actually out, nationally televised Fever games dropped from an average of 1.81 million viewers to 847,000, a 53% collapse. The 2025 All-Star Game, which she missed, drew 2.19 million, down 36% from the 3.44 million a year earlier when she played. By the time the full season closed out in September, the same season-long ESPN number that had been "up 21%" in July had cooled to up 6% year over year. The stat didn't hold up. It just got measured at a flattering moment and nobody went back to check it.

The spin itself isn't the tell, everybody in sports PR spins a number when they can. The tell is that nobody had to walk this one back. A business the market disciplines pays for a story that falls apart: sponsors get skittish, the stock takes it on the chin, someone answers for it. Here, the check was already committed regardless of whether the 21% held up past July.

What does hold up: the league has genuinely started building other draws. The expansion Golden State Valkyries sold out every home game in their debut season and posted the league's best per-game attendance average in 2025, entirely independent of anything happening in Indiana. Paige Bueckers and Angel Reese move real numbers of their own. The honest version of the counter-argument isn't "the league doesn't need Clark." It's "the league is trying, with real but partial success, to build more than one Clark-sized reason to watch", a narrower and more defensible claim than the one that got repeated everywhere last summer.

This doesn't change the underlying pattern: a league that could afford, financially and competitively, to treat its most bankable asset as replaceable kept choosing to, right up until the backlash made the bill too public to ignore. A business with no cushion doesn't get to make that choice twice in a row and still be standing.

## Happy Birthday America!

Two hundred fifty years in, the American theory is still that the market punishes you for mistreating the thing that makes you money, and a subsidy is what lets you skip the punishment for a while. The WNBA has been skipping it for coming up on thirty years now. That's either a remarkably patient investment or a remarkably durable way to avoid the discipline every other business in this country has to live under. 

It really is why the reaction from the public gets so visceral.  It's anti-thetical to the American Way.  You don't get to keep making big, public mistakes and then get bailed out financially.  There is no right to play professional basketball or keep a professional basketball league running.  The market has said protect Caitlin Clark, market Caitlin Clark, and let the tide of success rise for everyone.  It worked with MJ and it's time for the NBA to teach the WNBA how to do it with Caitlin Clark.
