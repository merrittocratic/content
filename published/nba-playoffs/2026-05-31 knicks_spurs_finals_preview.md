# The One-Job Rule

San Antonio is in the NBA Finals.

Take a second with that. The Spurs haven't been here since 2014. Two years ago they were 22-60. The pre-season review that had them as a Finals team would have been filed under fan fiction by Labor Day. And yet here we are, and the Knicks have a problem nobody was fully prepared to write about.

There is one thing to establish before the data: both teams playing in June are meaningfully different from the teams that played in December. Karl-Anthony Towns averaged 3.1 assists per game in the regular season. He is averaging 6.1 in the playoffs, more than double, after asking Mike Brown for a new role mid-first-round and turning it into a juggernaut. Dylan Harper wasn't a consistent playoff factor in December. He was in WCF Game 1. The December receipts are a foundation, not a final answer.

With that caveat noted, here is what they actually show.

## The Blueprint Has KAT's Name On It

The Knicks and Spurs played twice before the Finals. New York won the NBA Cup Final 124-113 in December, San Antonio took a regular season rematch 134-132 on New Year's Eve. In both games, Karl-Anthony Towns fouled out. The playmaker transformation doesn't change that fact, because the foul pattern predates it. Towns fouled out before he became the offensive hub. He fouled out before the Knicks were 7-and-0 when he recorded five or more assists. He fouled out because San Antonio runs a screening-heavy offense designed to generate exactly the contact situations that accumulate for a big man who gambles on switches.

Towns is averaging 3.8 fouls per game this postseason. The Knicks are a different team when he sits, the half-court spacing that makes their offense functional compresses and Brunson becomes far more ball-dominant at the end of a deep playoff run. San Antonio's coaching staff has a documented playbook that has worked twice. Whether they can execute it in a Finals series, against a KAT who is now a different and more disciplined player, is the first variable to watch.

## The One-Job Rule

Here is the finding that came out of the WCF data, and the one that will travel directly into the Knicks series.

Stephon Castle was the primary defender on Shai Gilgeous-Alexander for all six games. That assignment held regardless of score, quarter, or game situation. Over six games, Castle held SGA to 39.2% shooting from the field -- genuinely elite point-of-attack defense against a two-time MVP. When you look at the game-by-game numbers, though, a pattern emerges that goes beyond one player guarding another.

When Castle scored 13 to 17 points, SGA scored between 15 and 24, shooting under 40% from the field. The Spurs won all three of those games. When Castle scored 24 or 25, SGA scored 30 or 32. The Spurs lost all three.

The mechanism is not that Castle suddenly becomes a worse defender when he scores more. His field goal defense stayed consistent throughout. What changed is the free throw line. In Game 5, SGA had 32 points on six made field goals. He went to the line 17 times, made 16. Castle had scored 24 points on 64% shooting while also trying to guard the best foul-drawing guard in basketball. That is two jobs. When Castle plays two jobs, SGA stops needing to beat him off the dribble. He just waits for the contact.

The pattern is clean: when the Spurs have two reliable offensive contributors alongside Castle -- Game 1 was Wembanyama at 41 and Harper at 24, Game 4 was Wembanyama at 33 with Fox returning for 12 and a double-double, Game 6 was Wembanyama at 28 and Harper at 18 off the bench -- Castle plays one job and wins it. When only Wembanyama is producing, Castle has to score too. SGA finds the line, and Oklahoma City wins.

Harper's health is the variable that determines Castle's job count. Fox is unreliable in this series. Wembanyama will not produce at 33 points every night. What keeps Castle at one job is a functional Harper. Which is why the next question is the most important one in this series.

## Can He Do It for Seven Games?

Earlier in this run, we asked whether Wembanyama had found his co-star. The Kareem framework came up: Kareem needed Oscar Robertson in Milwaukee, then Magic Johnson in Los Angeles, before championships arrived. One game into the WCF, Harper answered the question in the most direct way possible: 24 points, 11 rebounds, 6 assists, 7 steals, the first rookie since Magic Johnson in 1980 to post 20 points, 10 rebounds, 5 assists, and 5 steals in a playoff game.

Then came the correction. Harper was hurt for most of the next four games. He combined for roughly 18 points across Games 3, 4, and 5 together. The Spurs went 1-2 in that stretch, and the one win came because Wembanyama had one of his dominant individual nights with 33 points.

Game 6 showed something different from the earlier injury games. Harper at less than full strength, coming off the bench in 22 minutes, still gave the Spurs 18 points, six rebounds, and four assists. A functional Harper, even at 75%, is apparently enough to keep Castle on one job. The question for the Finals is whether he can sustain that across a 7-game series against a team that has more defensive versatility than Oklahoma City, and against a matchup problem that is different in kind.

Brunson is not SGA. He draws fouls too, but through a different mechanism -- pullup jumpers in traffic, contact at the rim, late clock creation. Castle guarding Brunson is a new assignment, not a repeat. The one-job rule still applies, but the test is new.

## The Honest Take

Our model does not produce Finals probability outputs, and if it did, the confidence intervals would be wide enough to drive a truck through. What we have is a single finding that traveled intact from the WCF data: when Harper keeps Castle on one job, the Spurs win. When Harper can't, they usually don't.

The Knicks are 7-and-0 when KAT records five or more assists this postseason. The Spurs are 3-and-0 when Castle scores 17 or fewer. Both of those numbers are opponent-specific and both are fragile. That is what makes this worth watching.
