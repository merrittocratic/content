# There Can Be Only One, Precedent

---

The regular season is a wrap and the play-in games begin soon.  I think *someone* will win a postseason award, but at this point you can't be too sure. The NBA playoffs officially start April 18th, and most NBA narrative creators have already made up their mind about Victor Wembanyama. He won't win the championship this year. The Spurs are too young, the West is too deep, the sample size on playoff Wemby is the same as the sum of Bluto's and Flounder's grade point averages, 0.0. The consensus isn't necessarily wrong, but the way people are talking about the narrative is almost more interesting than whether it's true.

Because underneath the "he's not ready yet" framing is a question nobody is actually asking out loud: *has a player like this ever done this before?*  We're not just talking about a young player, or a good player making a surprising run. This is a generational **big man**, in year three, carrying a 60-plus-win team into the playoffs for the first time.  Has this specific thing happened, and what happened when it did?

There is one *real* answer. It has two chapters.  And, ZERO mentions.

## Chapter One: Milwaukee

In 1969, the Milwaukee Bucks used the first overall pick on a 22-year-old center out of UCLA named Lew Alcindor. He won Rookie of the Year. The Bucks made the playoffs and lost. Not what I'm referencing.  Now, in year two, they went out and got Alcindor a co-star: a 31-year-old future Hall of Famer, one of the greatest point guards in NBA history, who was the *first* player to average a triple-double in a season, Oscar Robinson.

Domination ensued, as Milwaukee went 66-16, ran off a then-record 20-game winning streak, and swept the Baltimore Bullets 4-0 in the Finals. Alcindor, who would take the name Kareem Abdul-Jabbar the following offseason, won the regular-season MVP, the scoring title, and the Finals MVP. This was only the third year in existence for the Bucks!

The wins are comparable. The age is comparable. The accolades are comparable. But, there's always something, and that something in this case is the last row.

De'Aaron Fox is a good player. He's averaging 19 points and 6.3 assists per game this season, made his second All-Star team as alternate this year, and has been exactly what the Spurs needed when they traded for him. While his performance dipped in this championship run, Oscar averaged 25.7 points, 9.5 rebounds, and 7.4 assists per game over his stellar career and is widely considered the most complete player in the history of the position. The Big "O" and De'Aron are definitely not the same player, and the distance between them is most of what separates "Wemby *could* win this" from "Wemby **wins** this."

Kareem needed the right co-star to make it happen, and Oscar fit the bill.

## Chapter Two: Los Angeles

Here's where the story comes full circle, because it doesn't just end in Milwaukee.

Kareem was traded to the Lakers in 1975. He put up enormous numbers, won MVPs, but the Lakers went nowhere in the playoffs. Four years of transcendent individual performance and zero championships, because they could never find the right co-star.

Then, in 1979, the Lakers drafted a 6'9" Spartan point guard named Magic Johnson with the first overall pick. In Magic's rookie year, his first playoff run, the Lakers met the Philadelphia 76ers in the Finals. Kareem's ankle gave out in Game 5. Magic, just a 20-year-old rookie, started at center in Game 6, played all five positions, and dropped 42 points, 15 rebounds, and 7 assists to close out the series. He won the Finals MVP. Kareem, becoming more of an elder statesman at 33 years-old, finally had his second ring.

Two co-stars. Separated by almost a decade. Both Hall of Famers, both in the conversation for greatest ever at their position. It took both to deliver titles to the greatest big man of his era, who some consider to be on the Mt. Rushmore of basketball.

## What It Would Take

Wemby winning a championship this year would be more historically unprecedented than Kareem's run in Milwaukee, he'd be doing it in his first playoff appearance, a year later in his career, with a younger supporting cast against a deeper conference. The "*it's never been done quite like this*" framing isn't hype. It's accurate.

A deep run in this postseason might be the most honest ceiling for Wemby and his Spurs. A Conference Finals appearance would already be historically elite for a player in this situation. And a championship, while not impossible, would require the "Alien" to do something no dominant young big man has done without a co-star on their way to the Hall of Fame.

Fox is good. Fox is not Oscar, and Fox is definitely not Magic.

That's not a knock on Fox, or on this Spurs team. It's just the only honest answer to the only real precedent.

The most interesting version of this story isn't what happens in April. It's whether the Spurs find their Oscar, their Magic, and what Wemby looks like when they do.
