# The Only Real Precedent

---

The NBA playoffs start April 18th, and the discourse has already made up its mind about Victor Wembanyama. He won't win the championship this year. The Spurs are too young, the West is too deep, the sample size on playoff Wemby is exactly zero. The consensus isn't wrong, exactly. But the way people are saying it is almost more interesting than whether it's true.

Because underneath the "he's not ready yet" framing is a question nobody is actually asking out loud: *has a player like this ever done this before?* Not a young player. Not a good player making a surprising run. A generational big man, in year three, carrying a 60-plus-win team into the playoffs for the first time — has this specific thing happened, and what happened when it did?

There is one real answer. And it has two chapters.

## Chapter One: Milwaukee

In 1969, the Milwaukee Bucks used the first overall pick on a 22-year-old center out of UCLA named Lew Alcindor. He won Rookie of the Year. The Bucks made the playoffs and lost. In year two, they went out and got him a co-star: Oscar Robertson, 31-year-old future Hall of Famer, one of the greatest point guards in NBA history, coming over from Cincinnati.

What happened next was a demolition. Milwaukee went 66-16, ran off a then-record 20-game winning streak, and swept the Baltimore Bullets 4-0 in the Finals. Alcindor — who would take the name Kareem Abdul-Jabbar the following offseason — won the regular-season MVP, the scoring title, and the Finals MVP. The Bucks won a championship in only their third year of existence.

The numbers from that season should look familiar:

| | Kareem's Bucks (1970-71) | Wemby's Spurs (2025-26) |
|---|---|---|
| Record | 66-16 | 62-19 |
| Player's year | Year 2 | Year 3 |
| Player's age | 24 | 22 |
| First overall pick | Yes | Yes |
| Regular season MVP | Won it | Frontrunner |
| Defensive Player | Dominant | DPOY frontrunner |
| Co-star | Oscar Robertson | De'Aaron Fox |

The wins are comparable. The age is comparable. The awards case is comparable. And then you get to the last row.

De'Aaron Fox is a good player. He's averaging 19 points and 6.3 assists per game this season, made his first All-Star team, and has been exactly what the Spurs needed when they traded for him. Oscar Robertson, in his prime, averaged 30.8 points, 12.5 rebounds, and 11.4 assists per game and is widely considered the most complete player in the history of the position. Those are not the same thing, and the distance between them is most of what separates "Wemby could win this" from "Wemby wins this."

Kareem needed the right co-star to make it happen. Robertson was the right co-star.

## Chapter Two: Los Angeles

Here's where the story gets better, because it doesn't end in Milwaukee.

Kareem was traded to the Lakers in 1975. He put up enormous numbers. He won MVPs. The Lakers went nowhere in the playoffs. Four years of transcendent individual performance and zero championships, because the co-star situation never resolved itself.

Then, in 1979, the Lakers drafted a 6'9" point guard from Michigan State named Magic Johnson with the first overall pick. Magic's rookie year, first playoff run, the Lakers met the Philadelphia 76ers in the Finals. Kareem's ankle went out in Game 5. Magic — a 20-year-old rookie — started at center in Game 6, played all five positions, and dropped 42 points, 15 rebounds, and 7 assists to close out the series. He won the Finals MVP. Kareem was 33 years old and finally had his second ring.

Two co-stars. Separated by almost a decade. Both Hall of Famers, both in the conversation for greatest ever at their position. And only then did the greatest big man of his era build the rings resume we remember him for.

## What It Would Take

Wemby winning a championship this year would be more historically unprecedented than Kareem's run in Milwaukee — he'd be doing it in his first playoff appearance, a year later in his career, with a younger supporting cast against a deeper conference. The "it's never been done quite like this" framing isn't hype. It's accurate.

The honest ceiling for this postseason is a deep run. A Conference Finals appearance would already be historically elite for a player in this situation. And a championship — while not impossible — would require Wemby to do something no dominant young big man has done without a co-star who belongs in a Hall of Fame display case.

Fox is good. Fox is not Oscar Robertson. Fox is not Magic Johnson.

That's not a knock on Fox, or on this Spurs team. It's just the only honest answer to the only real precedent.

The most interesting version of this story isn't what happens in April. It's whether the Spurs find their Robertson, their Magic — and what Wemby looks like when they do.
