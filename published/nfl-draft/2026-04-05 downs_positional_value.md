# What makes a consensus, elite player slide?  Caleb Downs is about to find out.

---

The draft has a positional value problem, and it's about to consume one of the best prospects in this year's class.

Three of the most universally praised players available this April play positions the NFL has collectively decided aren't worth premium draft capital: a running back (Jeremiyah Love), a linebacker (Sonny Styles), and a safety (Caleb Downs). What makes this year unusual is that the draft analysts have spent the last month bending their own rules to talk Love and Styles into the top five anyway. Love isn't a running back, he's a "weapon." Styles isn't a linebacker, he's a "defensive chess piece."  Did you see their 40 times?  Did you see Styles’ broad jump distance?

Downs, on the other hand? He's just a safety.

Same tier of prospect. Same "non-premium" label. Different treatment. We ran the numbers on 15 years of first-round picks, and the results tell a story the draft-industrial complex doesn't want to hear.

## The consensus

Let's be fair to the argument before we take a crack at it.

Safety is one of the least-valued positions on draft night. Over the last decade, only six safeties have been selected in the first round. Zero have gone in the top five. The highest was Kyle Hamilton at 14th overall in 2022, and that was considered a mild slide at the time — most evaluators had him in the top 10 on pure talent.

Daniel Jeremiah (DJ) — who has Downs as his top safety and a top-10 overall talent — said it plainly on Dallas radio last week: "*Weird stuff happens in the safety position.*" He pointed to Downs being under six feet with short arms as reasons teams might pass. The subtext was clear: the position depresses the pick, regardless of the player.

This isn't just media chatter. It's reflected in how NFL front offices actually spend. The analytics community has built surplus value curves showing that safety produces one of the weakest returns on first-round investment. The argument goes: you can find comparable talent at the position in round two or later, so spending a top-15 pick on a safety is an inefficient allocation of draft capital. Use that pick on an edge rusher or an offensive tackle, where the surplus value curve is steepest.

It's a clean argument. It's intellectually coherent. And it has a blind spot the size of Kyle Hamilton's contract.

## How we measured this

Before we get to the numbers, a quick explanation of the ruler, because the way you measure "good" turns out to matter a lot.

We used Approximate Value (AV), a stat created by Pro Football Reference that puts a single number on a player's season, regardless of position. Think of it like a universal currency for football production. A quarterback who throws for 4,000 yards and 30 touchdowns earns a high AV. A safety who racks up 100 tackles, three interceptions, and plays every snap also earns a high AV. It's not perfect — no single number can capture everything a football player does — but it's the best tool available for comparing across positions, and it's the standard in draft analytics research.

Here's the important part. We're not measuring raw production. We're measuring whether a player outperformed or underperformed *what his position historically produces at that draft slot*. A safety drafted 14th isn't being compared to a quarterback drafted 14th — he's being compared to other safeties drafted in the same range. That way a safety doesn't get penalized for accumulating AV differently than a running back, and a running back doesn't get credit just for touching the ball 250 times a year.

For each first-round pick from 2006 through 2020, we computed the gap between what the player actually produced in his first four seasons and what his draft slot historically yields for that position. Players who significantly outperformed got labeled a boom. Players who significantly underperformed got labeled a bust. Everyone else landed where you'd expect.

Then we sorted by position.

All of this might sound like it supports the case for letting Downs slide. If safeties aren't outperforming their draft slot at a higher rate than other positions, why rush to draft one?

Because look at what the NFL *does* consider worthy of a premium pick.

Cornerbacks bust at 27.1% — more than one in four first-round corners significantly underperform their draft slot. The boom rate is just 18.6%. That means first-round corners are nearly 50% more likely to bust than to boom. And yet teams draft corners in the top 15 every single year without blinking.  Paging C.J. Henderson, please pick up the red courtesy phone.

Edge rushers and interior defensive linemen, the "premium" defensive positions teams are most comfortable investing in, bust at 23.9%. Nearly one in four.

Quarterbacks bust at 25.0%. One in four.  Really, this is inflated based on some historic quarteback classes in the 2017-2020 classes

So the safety bust rate of 17% is lower than corners. Lower than edge rushers. Lower than quarterbacks. The NFL will absorb a one-in-four failure rate at each of those positions without hesitation, but a one-in-six failure rate at safety is somehow too risky for the top 10?

The discount Downs is getting isn't justified by the data. It's justified by habit.

## The Hamilton precedent

Full disclosure: I'm an Ohio State fan. So you can factor that in however you want. But the argument I'm making works regardless of what jersey Downs wore, because it's about the market, not the player. And the market already proved itself wrong on this exact question, with this exact profile, four years ago.

In 2022, Kyle Hamilton was the consensus best safety prospect since Eric Berry. Three-time All-American at Notre Dame. Elite size, elite instincts, elite versatility. Most evaluators had him in the top 10 on talent alone.

He went 14th. Thirteen teams passed.

Three years later, Hamilton signed the largest safety contract in NFL history — four years, $100.4 million, with $82 million guaranteed. He'd made three consecutive Pro Bowls, earned two All-Pro selections, and been ranked as the ninth-best player in the entire NFL by Pro Football Focus. His general manager called him "a unicorn."

Thirteen teams had the chance to draft a unicorn. Every single one of them let positional value math override what the tape was showing them. I'm certain the Giants would love a Delorean with a flux capacitor right now, they passed on Hamilton twice.  The Ravens got him at a discount and are now paying him like a top-10 player at any position, because that's what he is.

Would anyone be surprised if the J-E-T-S took Bailey or Reese at two and then Ty Simpson at 16.  Another New York team passing on a can't miss prospect.  Supposedly, Downs won't fall past the Cowboys, but as DJ previously said, "*Weird stuff happens in the safety position.*"

## The Track Record

The Hamilton story gets the most attention because it's the most recent. But it's not an isolated data point.

Eric Berry went fifth overall to the Kansas City Chiefs in 2010. The pick drew the same skepticism Downs is getting now same arguments, position, same positional value arguments, different decade. Berry went on to make five Pro Bowls, earn three All-Pro honors, and battle back from Hodgkin's lymphoma to become one of the most beloved players in Chiefs history. By the yardstick this analysis uses, he's one of the clearest booms in the entire dataset: a top-five pick who delivered top-five value over four seasons and well beyond. 

Harrison Smith is the quieter case. The Vikings took him 29th overall in 2012, the kind of pick that doesn't move the needle on draft night. Smith went on to become arguably the best safety of his generation, earning multiple Pro Bowl selections across more than a decade in Minnesota. The model classifies him as a boom at pick 29: he significantly outperformed what that draft slot historically produces. The late-round discount made the surplus value larger, not smaller.  Teams that waited for the position to come to them got exactly what the data predicts.  Oh, by the way Harrison Smith just retired, so do you think Minnesota will hesitate to draft Downs if the Jets pass on him twice?

Hamilton at 14. Berry at 5. Smith at 29. Three different eras. Three different draft slots. One result.

The metric is also honest about the misses.

Mark Barron went seventh overall to Tampa Bay in the same 2012 draft as Harrison Smith. He never justified the slot as a safety, struggled in coverage against NFL athleticism, and eventually converted to linebacker with the Rams. The model classifies him as a bust: a top-10 pick who meaningfully underperformed what that slot should produce. He's real, he counts, and he's part of why the safety bust rate in our dataset sits at 17.2% rather than zero.

But look at what Barron's case actually illustrates. The concern wasn't that he was a safety, it was that he was a below-average athlete at the position, asked to do things his athleticism couldn't support in the NFL. The concern with Downs runs in the opposite direction entirely: too small, too short-armed — measurables that ignore three years of elite production against SEC and Big Ten competition with athleticism that has never been the question.

The data doesn't say every safety is a safe pick. It says elite safeties are. The consensus already agrees on which category Downs belongs to.

## The prospect

We've talked a lot about how we can learn from the past to predict future successes or failures, but let's talk now about the actual player in question.  Caleb Downs played 44 career games across three seasons at Alabama and Ohio State, and **started** every single one of them. He finished with 257 tackles, 16 tackles for loss, six interceptions, 12 pass deflections, three forced fumbles, and two punt return touchdowns. He led Alabama in tackles as a true freshman, the in program history, by the way. I have heard that Alabama has pretty good track record at football.  So, to be the first to do it at that school may be a better data point than arm length.  He earned SEC Freshman of the Year, then transferred to Ohio State after Saban retired and earned unanimous All-American honors in back-to-back seasons. He won a national championship with the Buckeyes in 2024. He won the Jim Thorpe Award as the nation's best defensive back in 2025, along with Big Ten Defensive Player of the Year and the Lott IMPACT Trophy.  

And if that weren't enough, how about this from Nick Saban who knows a thing or two about coaching Defensive Backs, "*This guy is one of the most complete people I’ve ever had the opportunity to coach. He’s a great person. He’s a great student. He’s a phenomenal football player — his competitive IQ, how he responds on the field and reacts instinctively, It’s in the top tier of all players of all time.*"

He comes from an NFL family. His father Gary played running back for the Giants, Broncos, and Falcons. His brother Josh catches passes for the Colts. His uncle Dre Bly was a two-time Pro Bowl cornerback.

Multiple evaluators have called him the best overall player in the 2026 draft class regardless of position. John Harbaugh — who coached Ed Reed and Kyle Hamilton — said he'd take Downs "in a second" and used the phrase "potential future Hall of Fame safety."

And despite all of that, the current consensus has him sliding to 10, 11, maybe 12. Why?  Because safety isn't just the position name, it's what NFL teams are after.

## The real question

This isn't about whether safeties are secretly the most valuable position in football. They're not. On average edge rushers and offensive tackles carry more structural importance to winning games in most schemes. The analytics community isn't wrong about that.

But "on average" and "most schemes" are doing a lot of heavy lifting. The question isn't whether the safety position is worth a top-10 pick in the abstract. The question is whether *this specific safety*, a prospect that a Hall of Fame coach says he'd take "in a second", deserves a 5-to-10-pick discount because of a label.

The data says the risk at safety is no different than the risk at positions teams draft in the top 10 every year. The bust rate is 17.2%, right in line with the field and meaningfully lower than most of the so-called premium positions. This isn't a gamble. The discount isn't a risk premium. It's a tax on the position name.

Teams picking in the 5-to-12 range this April have a chance to do what the Ravens did in 2022: draft a generational safety talent at a price created entirely by positional prejudice.  The team that gets him at pick 10, 12, or beyond is getting a top-five talent at a mid-first-round price.

Hamilton went 14th and became the highest-paid safety in history. Every team that passed on him now wishes they hadn't.

Nobody here is saying Downs will be the best player in the draft. What we are saying, he might be the most *underpriced* player in the draft, and the market is penalizing him for it.

All this, and we haven't even gotten to what the data says about the front seven, where the positions teams feel most comfortable spending draft capital carry risk profiles that would make a stock analyst wince. The defensive side of the board has some surprises in it.  Coming soon!
