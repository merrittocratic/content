# Everyone Agrees Caleb Downs Is Elite. The NFL Will Let Him Slide Anyway.

---

The 2026 NFL Draft has a positional value problem, and it's about to eat one of the best prospects in the class.

Three of the most universally praised players available this April play positions the NFL has collectively decided aren't worth premium draft capital: a running back (Jeremiyah Love), a linebacker (Sonny Styles), and a safety (Caleb Downs). The draft-industrial complex has spent the last month bending its own rules to talk Love and Styles into the top five anyway. Love isn't a running back, he's a "weapon." Styles isn't a linebacker, he's a "defensive chess piece."

Downs? He's just a safety.

Same tier of prospect. Same "non-premium" label. Different treatment. And the data says the NFL has this exactly backwards.

## The consensus

Let's be fair to the argument before we dismantle it.

Safety is one of the least-valued positions on draft night. Over the last decade, only six safeties have been selected in the first round. Zero have gone in the top five. The highest was Kyle Hamilton at 14th overall in 2022, and that was considered a mild slide at the time — most evaluators had him in the top 10 on pure talent.

Daniel Jeremiah — who has Downs as his top safety and a top-10 overall talent — said it plainly on Dallas radio last week: "Weird stuff happens in the safety position." He pointed to Downs being under six feet with short arms as reasons teams might pass. The subtext was clear: the position depresses the pick, regardless of the player.

This isn't just media chatter. It's reflected in how NFL front offices actually spend. The analytics community has built surplus value curves showing that safety produces one of the weakest returns on first-round investment. The argument goes: you can find comparable talent at the position in round two or later, so spending a top-15 pick on a safety is an inefficient allocation of draft capital. Use that pick on an edge rusher or an offensive tackle, where the surplus value curve is steepest.

It's a clean argument. It's intellectually coherent. And it has a blind spot the size of Kyle Hamilton's contract.

Defensive backs — combining safeties and cornerbacks — have one of the lowest bust rates and one of the highest boom rates of any position group in the first round. Edge rushers and wide receivers, positions the NFL is perfectly happy to draft in the top 10, bust at significantly higher rates. Running backs are the worst of all.

The final column is the one that should end the debate. The gap between a position's boom rate and its bust rate tells you how much more likely a first-round pick is to significantly outperform than to significantly underperform. DBs sit near the top. Running backs — the position the draft discourse is currently talking into the top five — sit near the bottom.

This isn't a quirk of our methodology or our time window. A Bleacher Report study covering 25 years of first-round picks (1986–2010) found the same pattern using Pro Bowl and All-Pro rates instead of AV: safeties led the entire first round with a 51.4% Pro Bowl rate and a 14.3% bust rate. A separate analysis from The Hog Sty, covering picks 11–20 from 2000 through 2024, confirmed that defensive backs still lead in All-Pro rate and Pro Bowl rate in that range. Three different studies, three different methodologies, three different time windows. Same answer.

The position the NFL is most afraid to draft in the first round is historically one of the safest picks you can make.

The reason is hiding in plain sight: teams are so reluctant to spend first-round capital on safeties that they only do it when the prospect is a can't-miss talent. The selection bias creates the result. First-round safeties don't bust because teams have already pre-filtered for only the most obvious hits. It's not that the position is safe — it's that the bar to get drafted there is so high that only the best survive the process.

And that's exactly the profile Caleb Downs fits.

## The surplus value trap

The analytics argument deserves a more direct response, because it's the one that actually moves front offices.

Surplus value curves show that safety has a flat return in round one — meaning the difference between a first-round safety and a second-round safety, in terms of career production, is smaller than the same gap at edge rusher or offensive tackle. Teams look at that curve and conclude: don't draft safeties early.

But the curve is flat *because* teams only draft safeties in the first round when they're slam dunks. You're comparing a self-selected group of elite prospects against a broader pool at other positions that includes more developmental picks and outright misses. The curve isn't telling you that first-round safeties aren't valuable — it's telling you that teams are already good at identifying which safeties deserve to go in round one. The sample is pre-filtered for quality.

Using that data to argue against drafting Downs is circular reasoning. "First-round safeties don't provide surplus value because we only draft the sure things" is not the same argument as "don't draft this sure thing."

## The Hamilton precedent

Full disclosure: I'm an Ohio State fan. So you can factor that in however you want. But the argument I'm making here works regardless of what jersey Downs wore, because it's really about the market, not the player. And the market has already proven itself wrong on this exact question, with this exact profile, four years ago.

In 2022, Kyle Hamilton was the consensus best safety prospect since Eric Berry. Three-time All-American at Notre Dame. Elite size, elite instincts, elite versatility. Most evaluators had him in the top 10 on talent alone.

He went 14th. Thirteen teams passed.

Three years later, Hamilton signed the largest safety contract in NFL history — four years, $100.4 million, with $82 million guaranteed. He'd made three consecutive Pro Bowls, earned two All-Pro selections, and been ranked as the ninth-best player in the entire NFL by Pro Football Focus. His general manager called him "a unicorn" and said the Ravens didn't take the responsibility of making him the highest-paid safety lightly.

Thirteen teams had the chance to draft a unicorn. Every single one of them let positional value math override what the tape was showing them. The Ravens got him at a discount and are now paying him like a top-10 player at any position — because that's what he is.

The Downs situation is the same question with a different name on the jersey.

## The prospect

Caleb Downs played 44 career games across three seasons at Alabama and Ohio State. He finished with 257 tackles, 16 tackles for loss, six interceptions, 12 pass deflections, three forced fumbles, and two punt return touchdowns. He led Alabama in tackles as a true freshman — the first player in program history to do it. He earned SEC Freshman of the Year, then transferred to Ohio State after Saban retired and earned unanimous All-American honors in back-to-back seasons. He won a national championship with the Buckeyes in 2024. He won the Jim Thorpe Award as the nation's best defensive back in 2025, along with Big Ten Defensive Player of the Year and the Lott IMPACT Trophy.

He comes from an NFL family. His father Gary played running back for the Giants, Broncos, and Falcons. His brother Josh catches passes for the Colts. His uncle Dre Bly was a two-time Pro Bowl cornerback.

Multiple evaluators, including the one at Sports Illustrated, have called him the best overall player in the 2026 draft class regardless of position. John Harbaugh — who coached Ed Reed and Kyle Hamilton — said he'd take Downs "in a second" and used the phrase "potential future Hall of Fame safety."

And despite all of that, the current consensus has him sliding to 10, 11, maybe 12. Because safety.

## The inconsistency

This is where the broader frame matters.

The same draft discourse that is currently justifying Jeremiyah Love in the top five — because he's "not just a running back, he's an offensive weapon" — is simultaneously comfortable letting Caleb Downs slide past 10.

Scroll back up to the table. Running backs have one of the highest bust rates of any position in the first round — and across every study we've found, their boom-to-bust differential is negative or near zero. The league just watched the Raiders take Ashton Jeanty sixth overall last year, and the analytics community is already calling it a misallocation of capital. Every piece of data that supposedly argues against drafting a safety early argues *more forcefully* against drafting a running back early.

And yet: Love is a projected top-five pick, and the loudest voices in the room are working overtime to justify it. Downs is a projected 10-to-15 pick, and those same voices are shrugging because the position doesn't carry enough value.

If you're going to override positional value for Love because he transcends the position, you should be printing to the podium for Downs — whose position has a better first-round hit rate, a better track record of producing All-Pros, and a recent case study in Hamilton that proves the market consistently underprices elite safety talent.

The rules are only being applied selectively. And that's not analysis. That's narrative.

## The smart play

Teams picking in the 5-to-12 range this April have a chance to do what the Ravens did in 2022: draft a generational safety talent at a discount created entirely by positional prejudice. 

Downs is not a gamble. He's a three-year starter across two elite programs with a national championship, two unanimous All-American selections, and a Jim Thorpe Award. His floor is a decade-long starter who makes your defense better from Day 1. His ceiling, by Harbaugh's own estimation, is a gold jacket.

The team that drafts him where the market says he "should" go — pick 10, pick 12, pick 14 — is getting a top-five talent at a mid-first-round price. That's not a concession to positional value. That's exploiting it.

Hamilton went 14th and became the highest-paid safety in history. Every team that passed on him now wishes they hadn't. Downs is the same question with the same answer.

The data says trust the prospect, not the position label.

## What we're not saying

Positional value isn't a fake concept. It matters. Edge rushers and offensive tackles do carry more structural importance to winning football games than safeties, on average, in most defensive schemes.

But "on average" and "most schemes" are doing a lot of heavy lifting in that sentence. When the specific prospect in front of you is a legitimate top-five talent on pure evaluation, and the historical data shows that first-round safeties at this caliber almost never miss, the positional discount stops being smart portfolio management and starts being a failure to update your priors.

Caleb Downs might not go top five. He might slide to 12 or 14, and some team will call it a steal. And in three years, when he's made multiple Pro Bowls and is negotiating a contract north of $100 million, everyone will agree he was the obvious pick all along.

The data already agrees. The question is whether anyone is listening.
