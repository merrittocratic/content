# The R-Word Nobody in the NFL Wants to Say

---

I’m talking about Running Backs… What did you think I meant? Last week we talked about how the league was about to undervalue Caleb Downs because Safety is not just the position, it’s the mindset of too many NFL front offices. This week, different position, same disease.

Jeremiyah Love is going to go in the top 10 of the 2026 NFL Draft. Maybe, even inside of the top 5. Todd McShay recently told Joel Klatt that Love is the first running back he’s ever had ranked #1 on his big board in 26 years of evaluating. Awesome news for Love! But then, in the same breath, he also said that he’s “not fully convinced” about Love at 5 to the Giants. So, “the best player on the board”, and the guy who put him there still isn’t sure it’s OK to put him that high.

Here’s the thing, Love should go in the top 5. Our model agrees, the data agrees, and history agrees.

The problem isn’t the pick. The problem is the language. To justify taking a Running Back in the top 5, the draft-industrial complex has had to invent a workaround. Love isn’t a running back — he’s an “offensive weapon.” A “joker.” A “matchup nightmare.” A “swiss army knife.”

He’s a Running Back. And it’s fine to call him that. It’s been fine for a long time. We just have to start admitting it.

## The laundering

In Goodfellas, Paulie didn’t want his name on the restaurant. He just wanted the money. So they ran everything through a front until nobody could trace it back to what it actually was. That’s what analysts do with the Running Back position label. The pick is a Running Back, but by the time it goes through the mock draft machine, it comes out the other side as everything but.

Let me show you what I mean. Pull up any top-10 mock from the last month and read the Love comments out loud. You’ll find some version of these phrases:

*"He's more than just a running back."*
*"He's a weapon you can move all over the formation."*
*"He's an offensive chess piece."*
*"He's not in the same conversation as other backs because of what he does in the passing game."*

Here’s what those sentences have in common: every single one is a defense against an argument that nobody is actually making. Nobody thinks Love is bad. Nobody thinks he’s a low-ceiling player. The thing being argued against is the position label itself.

We’ve quoted McShay, so we might as well quote his older, alter ego. “He’s so much more than just a running back” — Mel Kiper, in ESPN’s collaborative mock, literally said: “I know, I know. Kiper doesn’t like early-pick Running Backs. But Love is so much more than just a Running Back.”

You only need to launder a label when the label is the problem. And the label is only a problem because the analytics community spent the better part of a decade building a case that Running Backs aren’t worth premium picks, a case the public draft discourse internalized so completely that even the analysts who love Love feel obligated to pretend he’s something else.

By the way, I really want to hear Jeremiyah go full TO and say, “I love me some Love…”

The laundering tells you everything. If Running Backs were genuinely fine to draft in the top 5, everybody would be OK with using the R word again.

## Where running backs actually live

[SCATTERPLOT IMAGE: love_rb_positional_value.png]

First-round Running Backs boom at roughly the same rate as first-round receivers, both sit in the low-to-mid 20s. This is the position the orthodoxy says you should never touch in round one. The data doesn’t agree.

But there’s a second number that matters even more, and it’s the one that breaks the “draft a wide receiver instead” argument in half.

Look at the y-axis (vertical axis). When first-round Running Backs hit, they hit bigger than first-round receivers — roughly 29 average 4-year Approximate Value (AV) versus 22. That’s not a rounding error. That’s an entire tier of production the “draft a receiver instead” crowd is leaving on the table. A great Running Back can carry an offense in a way that a great receiver, no matter how dominant, structurally cannot.

Nearly the same boom rate. Seven more points of AV when it hits. And a position the market is currently discounting relative to what the data says it should be worth.

That’s not “non-premium.” That’s a market inefficiency hiding behind a vocabulary problem.

## The bust list everyone keeps citing

The case against drafting Running Backs in the first round is built almost entirely on a small group of names that get repeated until they sound like a category. Trent Richardson. Rashaad Penny. Najee Harris. Sometimes Ronald Jones. Sometimes Mark Ingram, depending on how aggressive the argument is being applied.

Let’s actually look at that list.

Trent Richardson went 3rd overall in 2012 and washed out by year three. He is the patron saint of the “never take a Running Back high” movement, and his bust was so spectacular that it hardened a generation of analytics writers. Real bust. Counts.

Rashaad Penny went 27th overall in 2018 and never produced. Real bust, also counts — but he was the 27th pick, not a top-10 swing. The cost of being wrong was a late-first, not a franchise-defining whiff.

Najee Harris went 24th overall in 2021 and has been… fine. Not a star, not a disaster, exactly the kind of Pittsburgh workhorse back the Steelers thought they were drafting. Consistently producing 1,300-plus scrimmage yards and 8 total TDs per year since being drafted is not ELITE, but it’s definitely not a bust. Coming out of Alabama, he actually had the combo of receiving and rushing yards the NFL is looking for in a chess piece. The “Najee was a bust” line was wrong before he was drafted and it’s still wrong now, despite his lost season with the Chargers.

Now look at the same window for receivers. Henry Ruggs, John Ross, Kevin White, Corey Davis, Laquon Treadwell, N’Keal Harry. These are all top-15 wide receivers from the last decade who didn’t work out. Some of them spectacularly. The wide receiver bust list is longer than the Running Back bust list, and the busts cost more in terms of expected production lost. The difference is that nobody built a movement around them, because the position carries social permission to be drafted high.

The Running Back bust narrative is built on three or four memorable names. The wide receiver hit narrative is built on Justin Jefferson and Ja’Marr Chase. Both narratives are doing a lot of cherry-picking, and the cherry-picking happens to align perfectly with what the analytics community already wanted to believe.

And yes, it was very hard to not list ALOT of Ohio State Wide Receivers there as **hits**, but I opted for a little more objectivity.

## What about Gibbs, McCaffrey, and Bijan?

If we’re going to be honest about the busts, we have to be honest about the hits, and the hits are louder than anyone wants to admit.

Christian McCaffrey went 8th overall in 2017. The pick drew positional-value howling from the day it was made. McCaffrey has been the most versatile offensive player in football for over half a decade, won Offensive Player of the Year, and is the kind of player teams build entire offenses around. His 2023 season in San Francisco was the closest thing the modern NFL has produced to a one-man offense.

Bijan Robinson went 8th overall in 2023. The Falcons were mocked relentlessly for the pick. Two years in, Robinson is averaging over 1,400 yards from scrimmage and is one of the few legitimately good things on a roster that has spent two seasons searching for an identity. Take the pick away and Atlanta is almost unwatchable. I mean, they’re bringing in Tua because he’s left-handed. Desperate times….

Jahmyr Gibbs went 12th overall in the same 2023 draft, and this is the comp that matters most for Love. The Lions caught even more grief than the Falcons did, because they had needs at premium positions and used a top-12 pick on a running back. Two years later, Gibbs is a Pro Bowler, a Super Bowl contender’s leading offensive weapon, and exactly the dual-threat archetype that the “weapon” framing is trying to describe when it gets clumsy. He runs hard, he catches everything, he scores from anywhere on the field, and Detroit’s offense runs through him in a way that would not be possible if Brad Holmes had listened to the analytics community and traded back.

These aren’t “exceptions that prove the rule.” They’re the modal outcome when a team picks an elite running back near the top of round one and the player is actually elite. The rule is the exception, and the exceptions are the rule. We just keep telling the story backwards because the story sounds smarter when it’s contrarian.

And here’s the part that makes the Love case specifically: every single one of those backs — CMC, Bijan, Gibbs — was also called a “weapon” coming out. The same laundering that’s happening to Love now, happened to all three of them in their draft cycles. The vocabulary trick isn’t new. It’s just the only way the discourse can let itself do something it secretly wants to do.

And the most recent test case is already on the board. Ashton Jeanty went 6th overall to the Raiders in 2025 and immediately put up 1,321 scrimmage yards and 10 total touchdowns as a rookie behind one of the worst offensive lines in football.

But, if you want to anchor on a single archetype for what Love is being drafted to become, look at Gibbs. Same dual-threat profile, same “can line up anywhere” framing, same skepticism from the same people, and the results will be the same two years from now.

## The prospect

Let me actually talk about Love for a minute, because the piece so far has been about everyone else’s arguments and not about him.

Love is 6-0, 215 pounds, ran 4.36 at the combine, and spent three seasons at Notre Dame doing things that don’t fit on a stat sheet. He averaged over 7 yards per carry as the lead back in a pro-style offense playing one of the tougher schedules in college football, that included a National Championship run. He caught passes out of the backfield, lined up in the slot, and ran routes that wouldn’t look out of place on a wide receiver’s resume. He’s the rare back who genuinely can do all of the things the “weapon” framing implies — but, and this is the critical part, the things he does best are still Running Back things.

He runs through arm tackles. He has a second gear in the open field. He has the patience to let a hole develop and the burst to hit it before it closes. Those are the traits that make Running Backs valuable in the NFL, and they’re the traits that translate. The receiving usage is a bonus on top, not the main course.

The framing inversion here matters. Love isn’t valuable because he’s a “weapon who happens to play Running Back.” He’s valuable because he’s an elite Running Back who also happens to be a useful receiver. Get the order right and the pick stops needing a vocabulary defense.

## The discount that isn't a discount

Here’s where the Downs piece and this one connect, and why they were always going to be a pair.

Caleb Downs is going to slide because the word “safety” carries a positional-value tax that the data doesn’t justify. Jeremiyah Love is going to get drafted in the top 10 despite the words “Running Back” carrying a similar tax — and the only reason it works is because the discourse has built a linguistic escape hatch that lets the people taking him pretend they’re taking something else.

Both situations are the same underlying problem. Position labels are doing more work in NFL draft rooms than the actual data supports. With Downs, the label is dragging him down past where his talent says he should go. With Love, the label is being scrubbed off his jersey so the people who want to take him don’t have to defend the position.

The honest move in both cases is the same: ignore the label, look at the player, look at the data, and act accordingly. Downs is a top-10 talent at a position with a perfectly defensible bust rate. Love is a top-10 talent at a position with a better boom rate than half the so-called premium positions. Neither of these is a hard call once you turn off the vocabulary filter.

## So..., Draft a Running Back then?

We’re not saying that you should go rip up your mock drafts, and put four Running Backs in the first round. There is no Ty Simpson comp in the Running Back group. No Bailey vs. Reese debates. Love is a one-of-one in this draft. The structural value of the Running Back position matters and the analytics community was correct to push back on the era when teams were taking a Running Back in the top 10 every other year regardless of the prospect.

What we are saying is that the correction overshot. The “never take a Running Back in round one” rule isn’t a rule, it’s a heuristic that worked as a corrective when teams were being undisciplined, and has now hardened into orthodoxy that requires some mental gymnastics to override, even when the player obviously deserves it. When the people who are right about the pick can’t say why they’re right without inventing a new word, the framework has stopped helping and started getting in the way.

Love is going to be drafted high. He’s going to produce. The team that takes him is going to look smart. And the post-draft analysis is going to credit the pick to his “versatility” and his “matchup value” and his “ability to line up anywhere,” when the actual reason it worked is that he’s a really good Running Back and good Running Backs are still worth drafting.

Just say the words. He’s a Running Back, and then draft him anyway.
