# The Receiver Class Everyone Is Selling

---

The 2026 draft has a receiver narrative. Deep class. Lots of options. Get in early. Carnell Tate, Jordyn Tyson, and Makai Lemon are all projected inside the top 15, and the consensus has decided this is a receiver draft.

Even the people selling it are hedging. Bucky Brooks: "I wouldn't say it's elite; I would say it's really good." Jordan Reid: "We don't have those star players. We have a lot of No. 2 wide receivers in this class." That's not a ringing endorsement of paying top-12 money. That's the fine print.

Here's what the model says.

## The four profiles

**Carnell Tate** is the best of the group, and he's legitimately good. Predicted z-score of 0.18, 20.5% boom, 11.6% bust. The SHAP chart shows why: he's the youngest prospect in the group relative to his position peers, he's a strong mover in space, and the Ohio State WR pipeline (8 drafted, 15.9 average 4-year AV) is one of the more reliable program signals in the model. The combination of youth, athleticism, and a proven development track earns him the highest predicted score of the four.

That said — he's a quality prospect in a down year, not a generational one. Daniel Jeremiah has him #7 overall. The model doesn't disagree with the ranking. It just thinks the market is confusing "best available at the position" with "elite." Those aren't the same thing.

**Jordyn Tyson** has the lowest predicted score of the group at 0.04 — and the SHAP chart tells the story clearly. The biggest single drag is his program pipeline feature: Arizona State's historical WR outcomes in the model window are pulling his score down harder than anything else on the board. His projected draft position (inside the top 10 on some boards) is also working against him — the model expects more from higher picks, and Tyson's profile doesn't clear that bar. Elite athlete. Real injury history. The model is skeptical, and the spread in where evaluators have him ranked is the skepticism showing up in real time.

**Makai Lemon** sits in the middle at 0.11. Same problem as Tyson: his program pipeline feature (USC, thin WR history) is the biggest negative driver on his chart. Missing combine data on speed and rush metrics doesn't help. The after-the-catch production numbers are genuine, but at pick 11 or 12 the model is calling this a coin flip — and coin flips at first-round prices have a long track record of not working out.

**Kenyon Sadiq** is a tight end from Oregon. He's projected around pick 19. His SHAP profile looks almost identical to Tate's: 20.1% boom, 11.9% bust, predicted z of 0.17. The top driver is his athleticism in the run game — he moves like someone who should be a skill position player, because he is one. Youth is the second driver, same as Tate. His program pipeline is a null (Oregon has no TE draft history in the model window), which the model treats as uncertainty rather than a negative.

ESPN's TE projection system — separate methodology, different data — has Sadiq as the highest projection in the system's history. Two independent models pointing at the same player is worth taking seriously.

## What the model is actually saying

Tate is good. The model agrees. Lemon and Tyson are coin flips at round-one prices — the model is cooler on them than the market is.

And sitting 13 picks after Tate, at a position that produces more surplus value when it hits, is a tight end whose boom/bust profile is identical to the best receiver in the class.

The teams paying top-12 prices are buying the depth the media advertised. The team that takes Sadiq at 19 might be getting the best receiver in the draft. He just happens to line up with his hand in the dirt.
