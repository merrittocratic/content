# F*** Them Picks

Les Snead made that phrase famous in Los Angeles. For a decade, the Rams' GM was allergic to draft capital — trading picks, flipping futures, mortgaging tomorrows to chase the Super Bowl that finally arrived in 2022. His philosophy was simple: if you have the players to win now, draft picks are just chips on the table. F*** them picks.

Thursday night in Pittsburgh, Snead did something that looked like the same spirit but actually violated the whole premise. With the 13th overall pick in the 2026 NFL Draft, the Los Angeles Rams selected Ty Simpson, Alabama quarterback.

Snead's old line was about not hoarding draft capital when you're good enough to win. Taking Ty Simpson at 13 is something different. It's spending premium capital on a projection pick for a team that already has Matthew Stafford, has a roster built to win now, and just watched our model spend an entire article explaining why Simpson's production profile doesn't survive contact with the data.

*We wrote that article.* Go find it if you haven't. The short version: since 2010, four quarterbacks have been drafted in the first round with fewer than 15 career starts. The only one who worked was Cam Newton, who rushed for 1,473 yards and 20 touchdowns while winning the Heisman and a national title. Simpson rushed for 93 yards last season. On a team that ranked 125th out of 136 FBS programs in rushing. These are not comps.

The Rams didn't f*** them picks. They f***ed themselves.

That's the line that will define Round 1 of 2026. Let's go through who else made a statement night.

---

## The Big Winners

### New York Giants

John Harbaugh arrived in New York with two top-10 picks and a mandate to rebuild. He came out of Thursday night looking like the most disciplined general manager on the board.

At No. 5, the Giants didn't overthink it. Arvell Reese had slipped from where most expected him to go — easily a top-3 talent — and they took him. At No. 10, acquired in the blockbuster Dexter Lawrence trade with Cincinnati, they added Miami offensive tackle Francis Mauigoa. A potential cornerstone edge defender and a starting-caliber tackle, both in the first round, both at value.

Our model on Reese comes with the chess-piece caveat we've written about: the deployment risk is real, and the players who beat the "versatile" label did it by eventually committing to a role. Harbaugh's track record developing defensive players in Baltimore is arguably the best argument that Reese avoids the Isaiah Simmons outcome. The Giants, more than almost any other franchise in this draft, had the organizational credibility to make this pick.

Two picks, zero reaches, legitimate position of strength. The best night of any team in Pittsburgh.

### Cleveland Browns

Cleveland entered Thursday with two first-round picks thanks to prior trades — and came out having maximized both of them.

First, they traded down from No. 6 to No. 9, dealing the pick to Kansas City and collecting a third-rounder and a fifth-rounder in return. At No. 9, they landed Spencer Fano, the Utah offensive tackle who graded as the cleanest prospect in the OL class by multiple evaluators. Clean here means low variance: Fano isn't the highest ceiling tackle in this draft, but his floor is high and his technique is pro-ready. For a rebuilding team trying to develop its next quarterback, that's the correct calculation.

Then at No. 24, acquired from Jacksonville in a prior trade, they took Texas A&M wide receiver KC Concepcion. Getting a first-round receiver with what was originally someone else's pick, after already pocketing capital from the KC trade-down? That's draft craft. The consensus grades on Cleveland are glowing and they're deserved.

### Tampa Bay Buccaneers

The Buccaneers had the 15th pick and a clear need on the edge. They got Miami's Rueben Bain Jr., the most conventional "pass rusher who will rush the passer" in the front seven class, and they got him at a price that makes sense.

Our front seven piece had Bain as the cleaner value proposition over the chess-piece alternatives precisely because there's no deployment ambiguity. He lines up on the edge. He rushes the quarterback. The bust risk in the first-round EDGE pool is real — we documented the structural problem — but Bain's profile at 15 is more defensible than Bain at, say, 7 or 8. Value is relative in this game, and the Bucs found some.

## The Jets: A Case Study in One Step Forward, One Step Back

New York had the second overall pick and two additional first-rounders by the end of the night. The scorecard is mixed, and it's worth understanding *why* it's mixed, because the reasons matter.

**Pick No. 2 — David Bailey, Edge, Texas Tech: Complicated**

Our front seven piece made the organizational case for Bailey over Reese at No. 2. The Jets' track record developing conventional edge rushers grades out better than their track record with hybrid linebackers. Bailey lines up and goes. The Simmons template — ask a versatile defender to be everything, watch him become nothing — is franchise institutional memory for this team. Bailey avoids that problem.

Here's what the model actually says when you look at his waterfall: predicted z-score of **-0.42**, which is not an endorsement.

The concerning signal is specific: Bailey's sack percentile is 95th in our training data. Elite. His overall defensive production percentile — total impact across run defense, pressure, disruption — sits at the 43rd. And the model penalizes the gap. Elite sack production without broader defensive impact is a known pattern in prospects who don't translate, because those sacks accumulated in favorable down-and-distance situations against college tackles who won't be on NFL rosters. The Jets are betting they can develop the rest of the game around the pass rush ability. Maybe. The model wants to see more receipts.

**Pick No. 16 — Kenyon Sadiq, TE, Oregon: Steal**

This one is clean. Sadiq was the consensus TE1 in a class where tight end has genuine first-round value, and getting him at 16 with a pick acquired from Indianapolis is exactly the kind of surplus value the Jets needed to generate. A versatile tight end at a position the market has historically undervalued at his draft slot. The Jets have been trying to find a receiving anchor at that spot for years. Sadiq's profile in the model is positive.

**Pick No. 30 — Omar Cooper Jr., WR, Indiana: Questionable**

The Jets traded back into the first round to get Cooper. He was widely mocked in the upper half of Round 1 and slipped all the way to 30. That late availability is worth interrogating before you spend a pick getting back into the round to secure it.

Cooper is a talented receiver and Indiana's program pipeline gets some credit — they just produced Mendoza, their receiving corps was good, and the surrounding infrastructure was legitimate. But trading up for a receiver who slipped for a reason, when you just paid No. 2 overall money for an edge rusher with model concerns and already have Garrett Wilson on the roster, is the kind of pick that looks exciting in April and confusing in September.

The Jets came out of Thursday with real talent. Whether the sum of the parts makes them better depends on whether Bailey's overall game develops, Sadiq gets the usage his talent deserves, and Cooper was actually a value or just a price tag that looked cheap because it was at the end of the round.

## The Model's Concern List

### Ty Simpson, QB, Los Angeles Rams (No. 13)

Already covered, but to put a number on it: the model's QB sub-model on Simpson carries elevated bust probability. The production markers we use — completion percentage, yards per attempt, program pipeline, starts — all came in below the boom threshold. Alabama's QB pipeline in our training window does not have the hit rate that justifies this draft position for a prospect with 14 career starts. Matthew Stafford is still there. The Rams have a window open. Using a premium mid-first-round pick on a developmental quarterback who may not see significant playing time for two or three years is exactly the kind of decision that ages badly.

We called this one before the draft. The receipts are public. Come find us in 2028.

### Max Iheanachor, OT, Pittsburgh Steelers (No. 21)

The consensus take is forgiving here — CBS grades it as a "set-it-and-forget-it" organizational pick, a development tackle parked behind Broderick Jones and Troy Fautanu. The model is less charitable. Iheanachor has just five years of football experience, which is an extreme outlier even in a draft class that includes players with short college track records. Our OL sub-model treats experience as a significant signal. Low experience offensive linemen at premium pick positions bust at elevated rates, not because they lack talent, but because the NFL game comes fast and development timelines are unforgiving when the position requires mastering complex assignments from Day 1.

Long-term depth pick for Pittsburgh, framed charitably. Likely Day 2 value at a Day 1 price, framed honestly.

### Malachi Lawrence, EDGE, Dallas Cowboys (No. 23)

The Cowboys made a deal with a division rival, letting the Eagles move up from 23 to 20 to take Makai Lemon. Dallas then used the pick they received at 23 on UCF edge rusher Malachi Lawrence. This is a two-way concern: they helped an NFC East division rival get a legitimate weapon, and they took a player the model views with skepticism.

UCF's EDGE pipeline in our training data is thin. Lawrence's production profile was strong in conference, but the model's program pipeline feature penalizes prospects from programs without a history of translating that production to NFL success. The data on UCF defensive linemen in the league is limited, and what exists isn't encouraging. Lawrence may beat the base rate. But the Cowboys paid round-one money to find out, after simultaneously making a rival better. Both outcomes can be problems at once.

### Keylan Rutledge, IOL, Houston Texans (No. 28)

Interior offensive linemen taken in the back half of Round 1 have a specific bust pattern in our data: boom rates compress, the cost is still first-round capital, and the position value doesn't justify the premium. Rutledge generated real buzz at the Senior Bowl — his one-on-one reps against defensive linemen were genuinely impressive — but the model is skeptical of IOL prospects valued primarily on pre-draft event performance rather than extended college production.

The Texans have a good team and legitimate offensive line needs. The pick might work out. It's the kind of pick where "might work out" is the best case when the ask is first-round capital.

## The Bottom Line

Round 1 of 2026 wasn't a clean draft. It was a draft that separated organizations with a defined philosophy from organizations that got pulled around by the board.

The Giants knew what they wanted and executed. Cleveland converted a trade into surplus value twice. Tampa Bay got their pass rusher at a defensible price.

The Rams took a developmental quarterback they don't need yet, at a pick range where developmental quarterbacks bust at elevated rates, on a roster with a closing window. The Cowboys helped a division rival while paying a first-round price for a prospect the model flags on program pipeline. The Jets made one analytically sound decision, one legitimate steal, and one head-scratcher, which is exactly the Jets outcome you'd predict.

The night's most honest summary: teams that went in with clarity won. Teams that got cute lost. Some of those losses won't be visible until 2027 or 2028, when the prospects hit their development window and the opportunity cost becomes real.

Les Snead's famous line was about not hoarding picks when you have the roster to win. The lesson he forgot to pass along: picking badly is worse than not picking at all.
