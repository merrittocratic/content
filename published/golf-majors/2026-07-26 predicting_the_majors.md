# Predicting the Majors is a Major Payne

---

Four majors, four winners: Rory McIlroy at Augusta, Aaron Rai at the PGA, Wyndham Clark at Shinnecock, and now Ryan Fox lifting the Claret Jug at Royal Birkdale.

Same story every time: the market's shortest number went home empty. Scottie Scheffler carried the best odds in the field at all four majors this year:  same guy, four different sportsbook boards, four different weeks, and zero wins. Fox went off 48th in my pre-tournament rankings with a 0.4% win probability. The entire Birkdale podium: Fox, Cameron Young, and Sam Burns came from ranks 48, 30, and 22. Meanwhile the guy every model on earth had on top finished T4.

Again.

---

## It's a Cinderella Story

Here's the thing nobody wants to hear about golf predictions: "favorite" is weaker than it sounds when it comes to Majors.  Tiger vs. The Field used to be a compelling storyline. It's just not the same with this era of stars.

Going into the Open, my model gave Scheffler the highest win probability in the field: 7.3%. That's what "favorite" means in a 156-man major, the shortest number on the board, but not necessarily an expected winner. Flip it around, and what you're really saying is the best active golfer on the planet, in peak form, has a 13-to-1 shot to win it all. Run that same math across all four majors this year and the odds he wins zero of the four come out to roughly 55%.  Not exactly an anomaly, but when you're trying to get into the content and prediction business and your supposed bell cow comes up empty, it's a bit frustrating.

A golf tournament is one four-day sample drawn from a sport with enormous week-to-week variance (roughly 2.75 strokes if you perfectly predict someone's ability that week).  Also working against you is that the deepest fields of the year compress everyone's chances toward the middle. The interesting part was never that the favorite lost four straight, that's kind of what's supposed to happen more often than not. The interesting part is what those four losses looked like up close.

## If You Ain't First, You're Last

Scheffler's finishes at the four majors: 2nd, T14, T4, T4. Never outside the top 14, but also Zero trophies.

Here's the stat of his season: Scheffler hit the ball better at the majors than he did the rest of the year — +2.56
total strokes gained per round across the four of them, versus +2.21 everywhere else. The separation came on the greens:



At regular tour stops, Scheffler picked up more than half a stroke per round with the putter. At the majors, he was a net-negative putter. Across the first three he was merely mortal — dead neutral on the greens, with one genuinely
cold week at the PGA: -0.42 a round, T14 in a week his ball-striking was good enough to contend. Then came Birkdale.

Birkdale is a better story round-by-round than as one flat number. His approach play carried three of the four rounds
— +1.84, +2.79, +2.06 — exactly the ball-striking billing. Putting is where the week actually turned: dead neutral
in round one, a two-stroke bleed in round two, then round three brought the only day all week his irons went cold
too, the exact round he fell out of the lead group. Round four flipped it back: his best putting day of the
tournament (+1.61) paired with more strong approach play, and a closing 67 that had him making a real run at it on
the back nine.  Couple this with a *fortunate* drop and a birdie at 17, then a missed try at 18 to keep the charge alive, and he finished three back.

The honest fine print: sixteen rounds of putting data across four events is still a small sample, and putting is the noisiest stat in golf. It's really two of four majors, the PGA and the Open, where the putter cost him strokes on net; Masters and the U.S. Open were fine. That's not a curse, and Birkdale actually argues against one: even in his worst putting event of the four, one strong day with the flat stick nearly got him back into it. The signal isn't that Scheffler can't putt at majors. It's that his margin for error as expected gets thinner than during normal Tour events, and when the putter has even one rough day mixed into four, like round three at Birkdale, and it's enough to turn a live shot at a fifth major into a T4.

## Misery Loves Company

Scheffler wasn't the only proven winner who whiffed at the Majors. Matt Fitzpatrick and Chris Gotterup are tied for the most wins on Tour this year, at three apiece. Across eight combined major starts between them, the best finish either one produced was a T10.

Fitzpatrick's line: T18 at the Masters, T14 at the PGA, T22 at the U.S. Open. Then at Birkdale as one of the four shortest prices on the board, the trendy pick after three wins and a T4 at last year's Open, he missed the cut for the first time all season. PGA Tour's own recap called it "a sour end to a solid major championship season."

Gotterup's line: T24, T10, T43, T18. Four different majors, same story, never inside the top 10, never really in the mix on a Sunday.

Cameron Young is the honest exception here. T3 at the Masters and runner-up at the Open.  Leaving him out of this section would be cherry-picking. But outside of the four champions and Young, the players carrying the best regular-season form in 2026 didn't get within shouting distance of a trophy. It wasn't just the market's shortest price who couldn't finish the job. It was basically everyone who'd been finishing jobs all year.

---

## So, You're Telling Me There's a Chance

If you build prediction models, major season is a humility machine. My model's headline calibration numbers held up fine against the big public projection systems this year, and it still put the Open champion 48th on the sheet. The lesson isn't to chase the Ryan Foxes: a model that had Fox winning would be a worse model, not a better one. The lesson is that in golf, "favorite" is a statement about probability, not destiny.

Four different sportsbooks, four different weeks, one guy at the top of the board, and four misses. He also wasn't traveling alone: the best active player alive and the hottest hands on Tour all ran into the same wall this year. For the favorites, mine included, the majors stayed exactly what they've always been: a major challenge to get right.
